\begin{abstract}
Sarcasm detection requires intent beyond literal wording. LLM generation can expand sarcasm resources, but evaluation must distinguish sarcasm-relevant pragmatic cues from source-related cues. A shared encoder supports both sarcasm detection and machine-generated text detection (MGTD), yet source-predictive cues may distort sarcasm predictions. We harmonize Chinese and English resources and construct DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup analysis, with prompt-specified style serving as the sarcasm label for AI-generated texts. On AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic, the baseline joint procedure's false-positive rate exceeds its sarcasm-only counterpart by 67.0 percentage points, a pattern we call class-conditional negative transfer. Within AI-generated texts, AUC remains 0.82--0.88, showing preserved ranking, while score distributions shift upward. The higher false-positive rates appear in both languages and persist when the same detectors are evaluated, without retraining, on outputs from another generator. Representation diagnostics link source-related features to the affected sarcasm decisions and show that removing one data-derived high-variance direction changes the subgroup gap. Exploratory analysis finds that predicted human-authorship probabilities do not consistently track LLM-judge ratings of human-likeness obtained without ground-truth source labels. These findings motivate source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup evaluation and external validation before detector probabilities are reused as quality scores or rewards.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sarcasm expresses an intended attitude that may differ from an utterance's literal meaning, so automatic detection depends on implicit contrast, communicative intent, and context. Errors can reverse sentiment predictions and disrupt opinion-mining or content-moderation systems \citep{tay2018reasoning,vidgen2019challenges}. Annotation and model behavior also vary with elicitation method, annotator perspective, domain, style, and language \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,casola2024multipico,jang2024csc}.

LLMs offer a way to expand sarcasm resources, but their usefulness depends on realizing the requested style without turning source-specific regularities into shortcuts. Evaluation should therefore separate source and sarcasm: both human-authored and AI-generated texts may be sarcastic or non-sarcastic.

A shared encoder with task-specific heads can reuse one representation for both predictions, but it also creates a route for interference: MGTD favors features that distinguish human-written from AI-generated text \citep{guo2023hc3,wang2024m4}, whereas sarcasm detection should reflect intent rather than source. The labels themselves do not conflict; the risk lies in how the tasks organize the shared representation. If source-predictive cues influence the sarcasm head, information useful for MGTD but spurious for sarcasm can produce task conflict and negative transfer \citep{mueller2022taskconflict,hu2022spurious}. This motivates testing how joint training changes sarcasm predictions.

Because source-predictive regularities and sarcasm cues vary across languages and corpora \citep{jang2024csc,casola2024multipico,wang2024m4}, we examine the transfer pattern separately in Chinese and English.

DualTest supports source $\times$ sarcasm analysis, with prompt-specified style serving as the AI-text sarcasm label. Against same-configuration sarcasm-only controls, joint training sharply increases false positives on AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic. We call this class-conditional negative transfer because the degradation is concentrated in one source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup rather than distributed uniformly across DualTest. The effect appears in both languages and persists when the same detectors evaluate outputs from another generator without retraining. Within AI-generated texts, sarcasm AUC remains 0.82--0.88, showing preserved ranking, while score distributions reveal an upward shift.

To connect decodable information with model behavior beyond probe accuracy \citep{hewitt2019probes,elazar2021amnesic}, we combine probes, task-head correlations, and representation interventions. Source-related features align with the affected sarcasm decisions, and removing one data-derived high-variance direction changes the subgroup gap, making its content and causal role concrete targets for further analysis.

Detector scores can also enter generation rewards: ViSP includes a DIP-derived sarcasm score in its PPO reward \citep{wang2026visp,wen2023dip}. This motivates comparing detector sarcasm probabilities with LLM-judge sarcasm ratings. Separately, because predicted human-authorship probability does not measure perceived human-likeness, we compare those two signals as well, using ratings obtained without ground-truth source labels.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We construct DualTest from Chinese and English resources and use same-configuration controls to identify class-conditional negative transfer concentrated on AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic. The effect persists across both languages and when the same detectors evaluate another generator's outputs without retraining, while representation diagnostics link source-related features to the affected decisions and identify a direction for further causal analysis. Comparing detector probabilities with LLM-judge ratings further illustrates the risk of treating them as measures of sarcasm quality or human-likeness.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Sarcasm Detection, Synthetic Data, and Multilingual Resources}
\label{sec:related-sarcasm}

Feature-engineered methods \citep{riloff2013sarcasm}, neural sequence models \citep{ghosh2016fracking,tay2018reasoning}, and pretrained language models \citep{khatri2020,du2022pali} have each advanced sarcasm detection. Performance nevertheless varies substantially across datasets, which differ in label definitions, domains, languages, and modalities. Label schemes may reflect intended or annotator-perceived sarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval,casola2024multipico}, while multimodal resources may use modality-specific annotations \citep{yue2024sarcnet}.

Recent studies evaluate LLM-generated sarcasm through predictive performance, diversity, linguistic properties, and human judgments \citep{majer2026multi}, and associate synthetic-data characteristics with shortcut learning and poor transfer to human-authored text \citep{mahato2026llms}. These findings motivate testing whether MGTD-derived source cues interfere with sarcasm detection in a shared encoder.

\subsection{Machine-Generated Text Detection and Authorship Verification}
\label{sec:related-ai}

MGTD includes likelihood-based zero-shot methods \citep{mitchell2023detectgpt} and discriminative models trained on paired human--AI corpora \citep[HC3;][]{guo2023hc3}. M4 documents generalization gaps across generators, domains, and languages \citep{wang2024m4}, whereas RAID stress-tests unseen generators, decoding variations, and attacks \citep{dugan2024raid}. These findings are consistent with reliance on generator- or domain-specific source cues, which may also shape a shared representation used for sarcasm detection. \citet{bevendorff2025} frame realistic LLM detection as open-set authorship verification rather than closed-set attribution; here, binary human-versus-AI classification serves as a controlled auxiliary task.

To the best of our knowledge, the interaction between sarcasm detection and MGTD under shared-encoder joint training has not been directly examined. Because source is the MGTD target but should not determine sarcasm, we test whether the shared representation preserves that distinction.

\subsection{Multi-Task Negative Transfer, Shortcut Learning, and Shared-Encoder Mitigation}
\label{sec:related-transfer}

In multi-task learning, transfer depends on task relatedness and data-distribution alignment \citep{zhang2023survey,wu2020understanding}, while gradient conflict can contribute to negative transfer \citep{yu2020gradientsurgery,liu2021cagrad}. Matched single- and multi-task comparisons show similar failures and suggest that models may infer task identity from input distributions \citep{mueller2022taskconflict}; other work analyzes functional specialization in multi-task Transformers \citep{he2021stemcell,li2023functional}. A complementary shortcut-learning account is that models exploit training-predictive features that fail under distribution shift \citep{geirhos2020shortcut,ye2024spurious}. Shared representations can strengthen such correlations \citep{hu2022spurious,chai2025spurious}, while additional datasets can introduce them and reduce worst-group performance \citep{compton2023morisless}.

Mitigation approaches either isolate task-specific parameters \citep{pfeiffer2021adapterfusion,pfeiffer2020madx,mahabadi2021hyperformer,stickland2019pals} or modify information flow within a shared graph \citep{wu2019sifted,pilault2021camtl}. Our comparison of a plain shared encoder with two intermediate-representation variants follows the second direction and examines subgroup variation in negative transfer.

\subsection{Detector Outputs as Evaluation and Reward Signals}
\label{sec:related-eval}

Reusing detector outputs as evaluation or reward signals creates a broader risk. LLM judges increasingly evaluate generated text \citep{zheng2023judging}, while optimization against model-based rewards can improve a proxy but degrade a gold-standard measure \citep{gao2023overoptimization}. High classification accuracy does not establish that a detector's continuous output measures style strength or generation quality. For example, ViSP \citep{wang2026visp} includes a DIP-derived sarcasm score \citep{wen2023dip} in its PPO reward \citep{schulman2017ppo}. We therefore compare detector sarcasm probabilities with separately obtained sarcasm ratings. In parallel, we compare predicted human-authorship probabilities with ratings of perceived human-likeness, two related but non-equivalent constructs.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:data}

Testing cross-task interference requires distinct source and sarcasm supervision, with both attributes labeled for evaluation. Here ``source'' indicates whether a text is human-authored or AI-generated, whereas ``corpus'' refers to the original dataset from which it was drawn. We combine the English iSarcasmEval data \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval}, CSC \citep{jang2024csc,jang2023intended}, ToSarcasm \citep{liang2022tosarcasm}, News Headlines v2 \citep{misra2023headlines,misra2021sculpting}, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus \citep{zhu2022corpus}. Because none provides both a source label and a sarcasm label, we map the original annotations to a common binary sarcasm label space and construct 44{,}862 instances (approximately 87\% English). Four resources then serve distinct roles: SarcTrain for sarcasm supervision, AuthTrain for source supervision, GenTrain for generation fine-tuning, and DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm evaluation (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{SarcTrain.} SarcTrain contains 10{,}000 human-authored texts for sarcasm classification (2{,}500 per language $\times$ label bucket). Three buckets are downsampled, and the Chinese sarcastic bucket is expanded from 2{,}396 to 2{,}500 by sampling 104 records with replacement. We then partition the dataset into five folds. Because oversampling precedes fold assignment, some duplicated records occur in different folds, as quantified in \Appref{app:data}.

\paragraph{AuthTrain.} AuthTrain contains 19{,}995 texts for source classification. Seed-level quality control excludes one Chinese SarcTrain instance, leaving 9{,}999 approximately language-balanced human seeds (5{,}000 English; 4{,}999 Chinese). The locally deployed model identified by the Ollama tag \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} \citep{deepseek2025r1,ollama2025deepseek8b} generates the AI counterparts using four language $\times$ style templates, with each seed's prompt style selected from its sarcasm label. After generation and quality control, 9{,}996 counterparts are retained, leaving three unmatched seeds and 99.97\% pairing coverage. Source and prompt-specified style are thus distinct, approximately balanced factors for source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup analysis (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{GenTrain.} GenTrain contains 2{,}428 texts for the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) used in \Sref{sec:eval}. From 5{,}257 eligible records labeled as sarcastic in iSarcasmEval and ToSarcasm, we sample 2{,}103 (40\%; 1{,}214 English and 889 Chinese). We then sample 325 additional Chinese records with replacement, yielding 1{,}214 instances per language. By prompt type, the set contains 374 rewriting, 839 response, and 1{,}215 comment instances (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{DualTest.} DualTest contains 4{,}664 texts for source $\times$ sarcasm evaluation. Its human-authored subset combines the official test sets of iSarcasmEval (1{,}400 English texts) and ToSarcasm (972 Chinese texts). After generation and quality control, 2{,}292 of the 2{,}372 human test texts retain AI-generated counterparts (96.6\% coverage). Human-authored texts retain their original dataset annotations, whereas the sarcasm label for AI-generated texts records prompt-specified style rather than independent human annotation of perceived sarcasm.

The four groups contain 823 human-sarcastic and 1{,}549 human-non-sarcastic texts, plus 779 AI-generated texts prompted to be sarcastic and 1{,}513 prompted to be non-sarcastic. Record-level checks find no overlap of SarcTrain or AuthTrain with either side of DualTest. \Appref{app:data} reports these checks and the language $\times$ source $\times$ sarcasm distribution.

\section{Joint and Single-Task Models}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Tasks and Architecture}
\label{sec:model-arch}

Given a Chinese or English input text $x$, the model predicts a binary sarcasm label and a binary source label, with all four label combinations treated as valid. The tasks share a single DeBERTa-v3-large encoder \citep{he2023debertav3}, allowing us to test how joint MGTD--sarcasm training changes the representation read by the sarcasm head. We compare three representation configurations and examine how subgroup errors vary across them. \Figref{fig:architecture} shows the RCNN configuration, whose intermediate module combines recurrent context with convolutional pooling for text classification \citep{lai2015rcnn}, a design previously evaluated for multilingual sarcasm detection \citep{yang2024influence}.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_architecture.pdf}
  \caption{RCNN dual-head configuration: a shared DeBERTa-v3-large encoder, an RCNN intermediate module (dashed), and two task-specific heads. The comparisons in \Sref{sec:exp} remove this component in the plain configuration or replace it with two learned residual projections between the task branches, while retaining the same encoder backbone and task definitions (\Appref{app:hyper}).}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The RCNN configuration applies a single-layer BiLSTM followed by parallel one-dimensional convolutions and global max pooling. With hidden size 256 per direction, the BiLSTM yields a 512-dimensional vector at each token. We concatenate it with the 1{,}024-dimensional encoder output and apply kernels of widths 3, 4, and 5, each with 128 output channels. Global max pooling yields a 384-dimensional vector $z$, which passes through dropout to the two heads.

The plain configuration, used as the shared-encoder baseline, sends \texttt{[CLS]} directly to both heads. The second configuration uses a module termed task-branch interaction projection, which forms learned 256-dimensional sarcasm and authorship (MGTD) branch vectors $h_s$ and $h_a$, and updates them as $h_s'=h_s+P_{a\rightarrow s}(h_a)$ and $h_a'=h_a+P_{s\rightarrow a}(h_s)$. Subscripts identify the branches, arrows give the mapping direction, and primes mark the updated vectors. Because each branch supplies a single key--value vector, all attention weights equal one, so the updates are residual cross-branch affine projections in which each $P$ composes the corresponding value and output projections. We refer to the resulting full model as the interaction-projection configuration. All three configurations retain the same encoder and task definitions (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:model-train}

We distinguish representation configuration (plain, interaction-projection, or RCNN) from training procedure (joint, sarcasm-only, or MGTD-only): joint models have two task heads, whereas task-specific controls have one active head. We compare joint and sarcasm-only procedures in the plain and RCNN configurations. Within each pair, they share the encoder, representation configuration, sarcasm data, folds, and sarcasm-specific hyperparameters. The joint procedure also receives AuthTrain batches, updates shared parameters from both tasks, follows a mixed-task schedule, and uses joint early stopping. Compute exposure differs: the control traverses the full sarcasm fold each epoch, whereas the joint procedure samples sarcasm batches within the mixed schedule. The resulting contrast characterizes the complete joint-training procedure.

Training uses task-homogeneous batches in a 2:1 MGTD:sarcasm ratio, approximating the AuthTrain:SarcTrain size ratio. Let $z_s(x)$ and $z_a(x)$ be the representations read by the sarcasm and MGTD heads, and let $W_s$ and $W_a$ be their classifiers; subscripts $s$ and $a$ denote sarcasm and authorship (MGTD). The two representations coincide in the plain and RCNN configurations; in the interaction-projection configuration, they are the task-specific $h_s'$ and $h_a'$ defined above. For active task $t$, $B_t$ is a task-homogeneous batch of input--label pairs $(x,y)$, and only its task loss is computed:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
\mathcal{L}(B_t)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}_{w}\bigl(W_s z_s(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{SarcTrain},\\[9pt]
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}\bigl(W_a z_a(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{AuthTrain},
\end{cases}$}
\end{equation}

where $\mathrm{CE}_w$ is sarcasm cross-entropy with class-weight vector $w$ (weights are provided in \Appref{app:hyper}). For the class-balanced SarcTrain dataset, the weighted loss assigns a greater penalty to errors on sarcastic examples. Cross-entropy is computed from logits, with softmax applied only for probability-based evaluation, and each task-homogeneous batch updates the active task head and the shared parameters. The encoder uses a lower learning rate than newly initialized modules. The sarcasm-head rate is fixed across configurations, and \Appref{app:hyper} reports the MGTD-head rates. Unless otherwise stated, binary predictions use a fixed probability threshold of 0.5 rather than a threshold tuned on DualTest.

For dual-task models, checkpoint selection accounts for both tasks. At epoch $e$, the early-stopping signal is the equally weighted sum of the two validation losses, each normalized by its first-epoch value:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stop}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}^{(e)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(1)}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(1)}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$ are the epoch-$e$ validation losses for the sarcasm and MGTD tasks on their respective validation sets. Normalization reduces the influence of differences in task-specific loss scales and learning dynamics. Single-task controls use their own validation loss with the same five-epoch patience (\Appref{app:hyper}).

We conduct five runs, each using one SarcTrain fold for validation and the remaining four for training, while AuthTrain uses a fixed 90/10 split. Each run reloads the pretrained encoder and randomly initializes new modules and heads. Configurations use the same fold assignments and random-seed values, although parameter initialization may differ across architectures.

DualTest is not included in any training or validation split for either task. Five-run standard deviations reflect joint variation in folds and initialization, which are not separately identified. Wilson intervals are conditional on the predictions of each separately trained final model and quantify uncertainty from the test instances, not uncertainty from training or text generation (\Tabref{tab:quadrant}; \hyperref[app:c8]{Appendix~C.8}).

For each configuration used in the subgroup analysis, a final model is trained on all applicable data with a fixed epoch count determined from the five validation runs, an independent seed, and no early stopping. DualTest is not used to train these models or determine their training duration, and the final models are not included in five-run aggregates. The exploratory analysis in \Sref{sec:eval} uses the final RCNN dual-head model (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\section{Experiments and Diagnosis}
\label{sec:exp}

After training, we use DualTest to compare encoder backbones and representation configurations, evaluate joint- versus single-task procedures, and analyze source $\times$ sarcasm subgroups. We report five-run aggregate comparisons and use separately trained final models for paired subgroup analyses and Wilson intervals.

\subsection{Backbone Comparison}
\label{sec:backbone}

We compare dual-head mBERT \citep{devlin2019bert}, XLM-RoBERTa-base \citep{conneau2020xlmr}, DeBERTa-v3-base, and DeBERTa-v3-large \citep{he2023debertav3} under one evaluation protocol. For DeBERTa-v3-large, we additionally evaluate the interaction-projection and RCNN representation configurations. On the full DualTest set, we report positive-class F1 for the sarcastic class in sarcasm detection and the human-authored class in MGTD.

\Tabref{tab:backbone} shows higher mean F1 for the DeBERTa configurations than for mBERT and XLM-R. The RCNN configuration achieves the highest mean scores in this comparison, reaching 0.6309 sarcasm F1 and 0.9301 MGTD F1, while the two added representation configurations show smaller standard deviations across the five runs than the plain large model. Because backbone family, model scale, and representation configuration vary simultaneously, the comparison does not isolate the effect of any one design choice. For the separately trained final RCNN model, sarcasm F1 is 0.819 in Chinese and 0.429 in English. These language-stratified values are descriptive because prevalence, genre, annotation scheme, and language vary together. \Appref{app:results} provides macro-averaged, human-subset, and language-stratified results.

Human-authored texts retain dataset sarcasm annotations, whereas AI-generated texts use prompt-specified style labels. Because each group contains only one reference class, we report recall for sarcastic texts and false-positive rate for non-sarcastic texts.

On AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic, false-positive rates are 86.3\% for the plain joint model, 40.9\% for the joint interaction-projection configuration, and 34.1\% for the joint RCNN model. The matched plain and RCNN sarcasm-only controls both yield 19.3\%, and the latter two joint configurations remain more than 45 pp below the plain joint rate.

On the same texts, 1{,}021 false positives occur only under the plain joint model, versus 7 only under its matched sarcasm-only control ($p<.001$). The joint RCNN model also has a higher false-positive rate than its matched control, with a smaller gap. The plain and RCNN pairs provide same-configuration contrasts, while the interaction-projection configuration provides a complementary comparison in which architecture and optimization also differ.

On the same subgroup, the MGTD head's human-authorship false-positive rate is 6.1\% for plain and 6.6\% for RCNN. Within AI-generated texts, sarcasm AUC against the prompt-specified style labels remains 0.82--0.88 across models, so the relative ranking of sarcastic and non-sarcastic texts is largely preserved. The joint procedure instead shifts sarcasm probabilities upward, causing many texts prompted to be non-sarcastic to cross the fixed 0.5 threshold. The subgroup failure therefore consists of an upward shift in sarcasm scores for AI-generated texts while their within-source ranking remains largely preserved.

The matched plain comparison makes the concentration especially clear: its non-sarcastic false-positive rate changes by 4.3 pp on human-authored text but by 67.0 pp on AI-generated text.\footnote{Percentage-point differences are computed from unrounded rates and can therefore differ by 0.1 pp from subtracting the rounded values shown in the tables.} The aggregate F1 decrease is therefore concentrated in a sharply localized source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup rather than distributed uniformly across DualTest.

\subsubsection{Representation-Level Diagnostics}
\label{sec:mechanism}

We next ask whether source information in the shared representation is associated with the higher sarcasm scores. A five-fold cross-validated probe on full DualTest representations predicts source from both the joint model (AUC 0.986) and its matched sarcasm-only model (0.974), showing that source decodability alone does not explain the joint-versus-sarcasm-only gap. In the joint model, texts with a higher MGTD-head AI logit also tend to have a higher sarcasm logit in the target subgroup ($\rho=0.519$). In the whitened representation space, the probe and MGTD-head source directions have absolute cosine similarities of 0.41 and 0.49 with the sarcasm direction. Together, the logit correlation and directional alignment provide correlational evidence linking source-related structure to the affected sarcasm decisions.

We then divide the frozen representations into two halves, estimating each intervention on one half and recomputing predictions on the other. On the evaluation half, the source probe has a baseline AUC of 0.982. LEACE \citep{belrose2023leace} lowers it to 0.589 but mainly reallocates errors between source groups: the AI/human non-sarcastic false-positive rates change from 84.8\%/32.8\% to 68.7\%/49.5\%, while their unweighted mean remains nearly unchanged (58.8\% to 59.1\%).

As a second control, we define the source-direction-orthogonal first principal component (srcORTH-PC1) as the maximum-variance direction remaining after projecting out the estimated linear source direction on the fitting half. We remove srcORTH-PC1 before evaluating the held-out half. This changes the AI/human false-positive rates to 66.7\%/54.1\% while measured source decodability remains 0.982, and none of the ten sampled random whitened directions produces a comparable change. The preceding correlations and directional alignment associate source-related structure with the affected decisions; the intervention separately shows that the subgroup errors are sensitive to srcORTH-PC1, identifying it as a target for further causal analysis (\Appref{app:repr}).

\subsubsection{Evaluation on Qwen3.5-Generated Texts without Detector Retraining}
\label{sec:crossgen}

Using the same four language $\times$ style prompt templates, we generate a second AI test subset with Qwen3.5-9B (Ollama tag \texttt{qwen3.5:9b}) \citep{qwen2026qwen35} and evaluate it with the same detectors, without retraining them. After quality control, the non-sarcastic subsets generated by \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} and Qwen3.5-9B contain 1{,}513 and 1{,}550 outputs. The plain joint-versus-sarcasm-only gap increases from 67.0 pp on the DeepSeek-generated subset to 83.4 pp on the Qwen3.5-generated subset. On Qwen3.5-generated outputs, false-positive rates for the joint RCNN and interaction-projection configurations are 22.1\% and 29.5\%, respectively, and remain below the plain joint rate. Thus, the plain joint procedure again has a higher false-positive rate than its sarcasm-only control, and the three joint configurations retain the same false-positive-rate ordering despite changes in magnitude (\hyperref[app:c8]{Appendix~C.8}).

\section{Exploratory Comparison of Detector Scores with LLM-Judge Ratings}
\label{sec:eval}

To test whether detector probabilities agree with independent ratings of generated text, we compare predicted human-authorship and sarcasm probabilities from RCNN with LLM-judge ratings of sarcasm and human-likeness from DeepSeek-V3 \citep{deepseek2024v3}, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite \citep{googledeepmind2025gemini25flashlite}, and GPT-5-nano \citep{openai2025gpt5} obtained without ground-truth source labels. We evaluate 600 paired outputs---one from base Qwen3-8B \citep{qwen2025} and one from its QLoRA-adapted version fine-tuned on GenTrain \citep{dettmers2023qlora}---for 300 prompts. Prompts comprise 180 Chinese topic-comment and 120 English rewriting tasks, so language and task are not independently varied.

In Chinese topic-comment generation, mean predicted sarcasm probability rises from 0.780 to 0.895 while the mean 1--5 LLM-judge rating falls from 3.99 to 3.80. Spearman $\rho$ between probability and the mean available rating is $-0.020$ in Chinese and $+0.338$ in English. Predicted human-authorship probability rises in both settings while rated human-likeness declines, yielding a pooled $\rho$ of $-0.212$. However, the within-condition values of $+0.03$ for base outputs and $-0.12$ for SFT outputs show that opposing condition means dominate the pooled association.

The predicted-sarcasm change also differs between settings, increasing by 0.115 for Chinese topic-comment generation but decreasing by 0.124 for English rewriting. Across the six comparisons formed by two settings and three judges, fine-tuned outputs receive lower mean sarcasm ratings in every case, and five differences remain significant after BH-FDR correction. Classification probabilities therefore do not consistently reproduce the ratings' text-level rankings or condition-level directions.

Because pairwise judge rank correlations are low to moderate, we report judge-specific results and use the ratings for comparison rather than as a gold standard. Replacing the RCNN detector with the plain detector changes the corresponding correlations by at most 0.02. The resulting disagreements motivate external validation before detector probabilities are reused as quality scores or rewards. \Appref{app:results} provides the protocol and tests, and \Appref{app:cases} presents selected cases.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We identify class-conditional negative transfer from joint MGTD--sarcasm training: on AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic, the baseline joint procedure's FPR is 67.0 pp above its sarcasm-only counterpart; score distributions shift upward, while AUC within AI-generated texts remains 0.82--0.88. The higher joint-training FPR recurs in both languages and on Qwen3.5 outputs, with the RCNN and interaction-projection configurations showing lower observed false-positive rates on that subgroup. Representation diagnostics link source-related structure to the affected decisions and identify one direction in representation space for further causal analysis, while the divergence between predicted human-authorship probabilities and human-likeness ratings motivates external validation before detector probabilities are used as quality scores or rewards.

\section*{Limitations}

DualTest combines human sarcasm annotations with prompt-specified style labels for AI-generated texts. Accordingly, false positives for AI-generated texts are defined relative to the style specified in the prompt rather than independent human annotation of perceived sarcasm.

The core joint-versus-sarcasm-only comparisons evaluate the complete joint-training procedure. Within each pair, the architecture, sarcasm data, fold assignments, sarcasm-head learning rate, and class weights are held fixed, while MGTD batches, mixed-task scheduling, shared-parameter updates, and joint early stopping are introduced together. Compute- and update-matched controls could help disentangle these components. Comparisons across architectures do not isolate architecture effects because model capacity and optimization also differ. The cost-sensitive sarcasm loss may also affect absolute recall and false-positive rates.

Because oversampling precedes fold assignment, some duplicated SarcTrain records occur in different folds and may appear on both sides of a training--validation split, introducing dependence into checkpoint selection, although these records do not occur in DualTest. Generation failures from \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} leave 80 human test texts without retained AI counterparts.

The representation analyses on one separately trained final model connect source-related structure to behavior but do not isolate a unique causal feature or characterize variation across training seeds. The comparison between detector probabilities and LLM-judge ratings does not include human ratings, and language is not varied independently of generation task. Future work should test these findings using independent style annotations, matched training budgets, human ratings, and evaluation across a broader range of generators.

\section*{Ethical considerations}

All human-authored texts were obtained from publicly released research datasets: iSarcasmEval, CSC, ToSarcasm, News Headlines, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus. The iSarcasmEval and CSC repositories display MIT licenses, whereas we do not redistribute human-authored text from sources without an identified redistribution license (\Appref{app:data}). We collected no new user data or recruited participants, and we did not perform an additional audit of the released corpora for personally identifiable information or offensive content.

All AI-generated texts were produced locally using publicly released open-weight models (\texttt{deepseek-r1:8b}, Qwen3.5-9B, and the base and QLoRA-adapted Qwen3-8B). They were used only for training and evaluation and were labeled as AI-generated. For automated evaluation, evaluation inputs, generated outputs, and rating instructions were transmitted to the DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, and GPT-5-nano APIs. Dataset metadata and ground-truth source labels were not transmitted.

Given the observed subgroup error and the absence of validation for individual-text authorship attribution or high-stakes deployment, detector outputs should not be used as evidence of authorship or misconduct without human review and domain-specific validation. They should not be deployed for content moderation or automated sanctions without evaluating out-of-distribution behavior. The error concentrated on AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic is an example of systematic subgroup error affecting a particular type of generated content.

AI-assisted tools supported language editing, structural revision, and formatting, but did not generate the reported experimental outputs or statistics. The authors determined the study design, analyses, and conclusions and take full responsibility for the work.
