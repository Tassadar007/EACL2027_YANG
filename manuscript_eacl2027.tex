\begin{abstract}
Sarcasm is non-literal language whose intended meaning may diverge from apparent sentiment. As a characteristically human form of expression grounded in implicit intent and context, it is difficult to detect automatically. LLM-generated examples could improve detection by augmenting training data. However, this strategy requires LLMs to produce text that both conveys sarcasm and resembles human writing, so sarcasm and authorship must be evaluated together. Although sarcasm detection and machine-generated text detection (MGTD) are usually studied separately, their interaction in shared representations remains insufficiently understood. MGTD must encode source cues, whereas sarcasm detection should reflect intent rather than source. We provide the first bilingual diagnosis, to our knowledge, of joint MGTD--sarcasm training in Chinese and English. We harmonize bilingual resources, generate topic-matched AI text, and build DualTest with source and sarcasm labels, defining AI labels by requested style. Compared with their single-task counterparts, jointly trained models are markedly more likely to misclassify AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic as sarcastic. Human-authorship detector scores and LLM human-likeness ratings also rank outputs differently. These findings motivate subgroup evaluation and caution against detector-only assessment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A sarcastic sentence conveys an attitude or intent that may oppose its literal meaning. As a characteristically human form of expression, sarcasm relies on implicit contrast, intent, and context, making it difficult to detect automatically. Errors can invert sentiment analysis and disrupt opinion-mining or content-moderation systems \citep{tay2018reasoning,vidgen2019challenges}. Annotation outcomes also vary with elicitation method and annotator perspective \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,casola2024multipico}, while model behavior is sensitive to domain, style, and language \citep{jang2024csc,casola2024multipico} (\Sref{sec:related-sarcasm}).

One potential response is to augment training data with LLM-generated examples. This strategy is useful only if generated text both conveys the intended sarcasm and resembles human writing; otherwise, source-specific artifacts may become shortcuts. Sarcasm and authorship must therefore be considered together. Human-authored and AI-generated texts can each be sarcastic or non-sarcastic, yielding all four combinations.

Machine-generated text detection (MGTD) imposes a contrasting representational demand: it must distinguish human-written from AI-generated text and thus favors source-predictive representations \citep{guo2023hc3,wang2024m4} (\Sref{sec:related-ai}). These stylistic or distributional cues may overlap with features used for sarcasm detection. The labels do not conflict; the potential conflict lies in how the tasks organize a shared representation. MGTD must encode source, whereas sarcasm detection should reflect intent rather than source. Shared-encoder multi-task learning can suffer task conflict and negative transfer by exploiting information useful for one task but spurious for another \citep{mueller2022taskconflict,hu2022spurious}. Separate models avoid this coupling but forgo efficient encoder sharing, motivating a direct test of whether joint training contaminates sarcasm decisions.

Detector scores also matter downstream because they can become generation rewards. ViSP derives PPO rewards from a sarcasm detector \citep{wang2026visp}. Yet useful generated sarcasm must satisfy two distinct objectives: conveying sarcastic intent and being perceived as human-like. A dual-head detector may appear to provide corresponding signals through sarcasm and authorship probabilities, but authorship probability is not itself a measure of human-likeness. We therefore test whether these detector scores track source-blinded LLM ratings of sarcasm and perceived human-likeness (\Sref{sec:eval}).

To test these risks rather than assume MGTD is a beneficial auxiliary task, we compare same-architecture dual- and single-head models on bilingual Chinese--English data and build DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup analysis. The evaluated joint-training procedure sharply increases sarcasm false positives on AI text prompted to be non-sarcastic; we call this class-conditional negative transfer. The direction persists when the AI test texts are regenerated with Qwen3.5-9B, while RCNN and interaction projection show lower rates than the plain configuration.

We then examine the shared representation. Source information is highly decodable and correlates with sarcasm decisions. To connect encoded information to actual decisions beyond probe accuracy \citep{hewitt2019probes,elazar2021amnesic}, we combine probes, task-head correlations, and representation interventions. These analyses identify a non-random high-variance direction to which the subgroup gap is sensitive, supporting a source-correlated shortcut hypothesis and providing a concrete target for causal analysis (\Sref{sec:mechanism}, \Appref{app:repr}).

\paragraph{Contributions.} To our knowledge, we provide the first bilingual diagnostic study of how joint training with MGTD changes sarcasm detection across Chinese and English, together with DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm analysis. Same-architecture comparisons across models and generators identify class-conditional negative transfer concentrated on AI-generated text prompted to be non-sarcastic; cross-architecture comparisons characterize how its severity varies across the evaluated configurations. Representation analyses support the source-correlated shortcut hypothesis and define concrete targets for causal analysis. Finally, an exploratory detector--judge comparison illustrates a downstream risk of using detector scores to evaluate generated sarcasm and perceived human-likeness.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Sarcasm Detection and Multilingual Resources}
\label{sec:related-sarcasm}

Feature-engineered methods \citep{riloff2013sarcasm}, neural sequence models \citep{ghosh2016fracking,tay2018reasoning}, and pretrained language models \citep{khatri2020,du2022pali} have each advanced sarcasm detection. The task remains difficult in multilingual and class-imbalanced settings, especially because datasets differ in whether labels reflect intended sarcasm or annotator-perceived sarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval,casola2024multipico}. Although scores are not directly comparable across datasets, languages, modalities, or evaluation setups, they illustrate substantial benchmark variability. On English iSarcasmEval \citep{abufarha2022isarcasmeval}, the top two systems report positive-class F1 of 0.605 and 0.569; across MultiPICo languages \citep{casola2024multipico}, zero-shot ChatGPT positive-class F1 ranges from 0.152 to 0.567. On the bilingual multimodal SarcNet benchmark \citep{yue2024sarcnet}, a text-only XLM-R baseline reaches 0.734 F1.

RCNNs combine recurrent context with convolutional pooling for text classification \citep{lai2015rcnn} and have also been evaluated for multilingual sarcasm detection \citep{yang2024influence}; we therefore include an RCNN intermediate configuration in \Sref{sec:model-arch}.

The benchmark results summarized above come from different datasets, so \Sref{sec:backbone} provides directly comparable mBERT dual-head baselines on DualTest. To our knowledge, existing sarcasm resources do not provide both sarcasm and human-versus-AI source labels. DualTest supplies both labels in a bilingual Chinese--English setting.

The Chinese and English source resources differ in domain, annotation procedure, and class balance, so bilinguality introduces additional distributional variation. We treat language as a robustness and stratification dimension rather than as the primary transfer objective: the study asks whether the subgroup failure is confined to one language, not whether knowledge transfers across languages. We therefore report language-stratified results in addition to pooled metrics.

\subsection{Machine-Generated Text Detection and Authorship Verification}
\label{sec:related-ai}

Machine-generated text detection (MGTD) comprises zero-shot model-likelihood methods, such as probability-curvature detectors \citep{mitchell2023detectgpt}, and discriminative models trained on paired human--AI corpora \citep[HC3;][]{guo2023hc3}. Generalization is a central difficulty: M4 \citep{wang2024m4} provides a benchmark across generators, domains, and languages and shows that detectors struggle on unseen domains or LLMs, while RAID \citep{dugan2024raid} stress-tests detectors under unseen generators, decoding variations, and adversarial attacks. This generalization gap is consistent with reliance on source-predictive regularities that are partly generator- or domain-specific; such cues are relevant here because they may enter a representation shared with sarcasm detection. \citet{bevendorff2025} distinguish closed-set authorship attribution from open-set authorship verification and argue that realistic LLM detection is better cast as the latter. Our binary MGTD setup is narrower: we use it as a controlled source-classification auxiliary task, not as a measure of text quality.

In our literature search, we found no work that jointly trains sarcasm detection and MGTD with a shared encoder.\footnote{Searches conducted in May 2026 and updated in July 2026 across Semantic Scholar, arXiv, ACL Anthology, and Google Scholar, using keyword combinations including ``sarcasm detection,'' ``machine-generated text detection,'' ``AI-generated text detection,'' ``multi-task learning,'' and ``joint detection.''} This gap motivates our study because source information has different roles in the two tasks: it is the target of MGTD but should not by itself determine a sarcasm detector's prediction. Our study tests whether a shared representation preserves that distinction.

\subsection{Multi-Task Negative Transfer, Shortcut Learning, and Shared-Encoder Mitigation}
\label{sec:related-transfer}

In multi-task learning, auxiliary tasks are not always beneficial: transfer depends on task relatedness and alignment between task data \citep{zhang2023survey,wu2020understanding}, while gradient conflict is a central optimization challenge that can contribute to negative transfer \citep{yu2020gradientsurgery,liu2021cagrad}. \citet{mueller2022taskconflict} provide a relevant NLP precedent through matched single- and multi-task comparisons. They find similar negative-transfer patterns in canonical and text-to-text systems and note that models may infer task identity from input distributions, a strategy that may not transfer reliably out of distribution. \citet{he2021stemcell} and \citet{li2023functional} further analyze functional specialization in multi-task Transformer encoders. Shortcut learning offers a complementary reading: models may exploit features that are predictive in the training distribution yet fail under distribution shift \citep{geirhos2020shortcut,ye2024spurious}. In MTL, shared representations can increase reliance on spurious correlations \citep{hu2022spurious,chai2025spurious}. Related multi-dataset work shows that adding data can introduce spurious correlations and reduce worst-group performance \citep{compton2023morisless}.

Shared-encoder mitigation either isolates task-specific parameters \citep{pfeiffer2021adapterfusion,pfeiffer2020madx,mahabadi2021hyperformer,stickland2019pals} or modifies information flow within shared computation \citep{wu2019sifted,pilault2021camtl}. Our analysis is closer to the second design direction: we compare a no-module baseline with two intermediate-representation variants and test whether degradation changes across source $\times$ sarcasm subgroups (\Sref{sec:quadrant}).

\subsection{Misalignment in Detector-Based Evaluation and Rewards}
\label{sec:related-eval}

The representational conflict studied above may also matter when detector outputs are reused beyond classification, particularly as evaluation or reward signals for generated text. Automated scorers, including LLM judges, are increasingly used to evaluate generated text \citep{zheng2023judging}. Model-based scores are also optimized as rewards, but overoptimization can increase a proxy score while degrading a gold-standard measure \citep{gao2023overoptimization}. A detector can achieve useful classification accuracy without its continuous output being calibrated as a measure of style strength or generation quality. Multimodal sarcasm generation may face this risk: ViSP \citep{wang2026visp} uses the DIP sarcasm detector \citep{wen2023dip} as a PPO reward \citep{schulman2017ppo}. As an exploratory downstream analysis, \hyperref[sec:eval]{Section~\ref*{sec:eval}} compares detector scores for human authorship with separately obtained, source-blinded LLM ratings of perceived human-likeness.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:data}

Testing for interference requires separate task supervision and evaluation labels for authorship and sarcasm. Here ``source'' denotes human-versus-AI authorship; ``corpus'' denotes an input dataset. We combine iSarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval}, CSC \citep{jang2024csc,jang2023intended}, ToSarcasm \citep{liang2022tosarcasm}, News Headlines v2 \citep{misra2023headlines,misra2021sculpting}, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus \citep{zhu2022corpus}. None provides both labels. Mapping their annotations to a common binary sarcasm label yields 44{,}862 pooled instances (approximately 87\% English), from which we construct three training sets and DualTest (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Sarcasm classification training set, SarcTrain (10{,}000, all human).} Each language $\times$ label bucket contains 2{,}500 instances. Three buckets are downsampled; the Chinese sarcastic bucket starts from 2{,}396 records and adds 104 oversampled records. The resulting set is organized into five folds.

\paragraph{MGTD training set, AuthTrain (19{,}995).} AuthTrain draws 9{,}999 approximately language-balanced human seeds from SarcTrain (5{,}000 English / 4{,}999 Chinese) after one Chinese seed is excluded during seed-level generation quality control. DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (Ollama tag \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b}) \citep{deepseek2025r1,ollama2025deepseek8b} produces 9{,}996 retained AI counterparts; three selected human seeds remain unmatched, giving 99.97\% pairing coverage. \Appref{app:data} details the exclusions.

Four language $\times$ style templates follow the balanced seed labels. Authorship and requested-style labels are constructed as separate, approximately balanced factors; no independence is assumed for realized textual features or perceived sarcasm (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Generation training set, GenTrain (2{,}428; used in \Sref{sec:eval}).} From 5{,}257 eligible positive-sarcasm records in iSarcasm and ToSarcasm, seed-42 sampling selects 2{,}103 records (1{,}214 English; 889 Chinese). Chinese oversampling adds 325 records, yielding 1{,}214 per language. The resulting set contains 374 rewriting, 839 response, and 1{,}215 comment instances and serves as the instruction-tuning data for supervised fine-tuning (SFT; \Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Diagnostic test set, DualTest (4{,}664).} The human subset combines official iSarcasm (1{,}400 English) and ToSarcasm (972 Chinese) test sets. Generation quality control retains AI counterparts for 2{,}292 of 2{,}372 texts (96.6\% coverage). Human texts retain their annotations; for AI texts, the sarcasm label records the prompted condition rather than an independently annotated judgment of perceived sarcasm.

The four source $\times$ sarcasm groups contain 823 human-sarcastic, 1{,}549 human-non-sarcastic, 779 AI-sarcastic, and 1{,}513 AI-non-sarcastic instances. \Appref{app:data} gives the language $\times$ source $\times$ sarcasm distribution.

\section{Dual-Head Detection Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Tasks and Architecture}
\label{sec:model-arch}

Given a Chinese or English input text $x$, the model produces two separate, non-mutually-exclusive binary predictions: whether $x$ is labeled sarcastic and whether its authorship source is labeled human or AI. Any combination of the two labels is valid. The tasks share a single DeBERTa-v3-large encoder \citep{he2023debertav3}, allowing us to test how incorporating MGTD into joint training changes the representation read by the sarcasm head. Encoder outputs are aggregated by an intermediate module and then passed to two task-specific classification heads; \Figref{fig:architecture} shows the RCNN configuration.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_architecture.pdf}
  \caption{RCNN dual-head configuration: a shared DeBERTa-v3-large encoder, an RCNN intermediate module (dashed), and two task-specific heads. The comparisons in \Sref{sec:exp} remove this component in the plain configuration or replace it with task-branch interaction projection, while retaining the same encoder backbone and task definitions (\Appref{app:hyper}).}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The RCNN configuration uses BiLSTM $+$ CNN as its intermediate module. A single-layer bidirectional LSTM with hidden size 256 per direction produces a 512-dimensional output at each token position. We concatenate it with the 1{,}024-dimensional encoder output, then apply three groups of one-dimensional convolutions with kernel widths 3/4/5 and 128 channels each. Global max pooling produces a 384-dimensional vector $z$. After dropout, $z$ is passed to the two heads, yielding a DeBERTa-based dual-head RCNN configuration.

To test the intermediate layer's role, \Sref{sec:exp} compares two variants. The plain model sends \texttt{[CLS]} directly to both heads. The interaction-projection model forms two learned 256-dimensional projections, $h_s$ and $h_a$, and updates them as $h_s'=h_s+P_{a\rightarrow s}(h_a)$ and $h_a'=h_a+P_{s\rightarrow a}(h_s)$. Each branch contains one vector, so no query--key interaction remains; $P$ is the effective learned value/output projection. The configurations retain the same encoder and task definitions while testing distinct intermediate-representation designs (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:model-train}

The training protocol supports controlled dual--single comparisons in the plain and RCNN configurations. Within each comparison, the models use the same encoder architecture, intermediate configuration, sarcasm data and fold assignments, and sarcasm-specific hyperparameters. The dual model additionally receives AuthTrain batches, updates shared parameters from both tasks, follows the mixed-task schedule, and uses joint early stopping; the comparison therefore evaluates the complete joint-training procedure rather than any single component.

Training uses a mixed loader that supplies task-homogeneous batches according to a 2:1 MGTD:sarcasm schedule, matching the approximate data-size ratio between AuthTrain and SarcTrain. Let $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ denote parameters updated by both tasks, let $z_s(x)$ and $z_a(x)$ be the representations read by the sarcasm and MGTD heads, and let $W_s$ and $W_a$ denote the two classification heads. The two representations coincide in the plain and RCNN configurations; in interaction projection they are the task-specific $h_s'$ and $h_a'$ defined above. At step $t$, the loss is computed only for the task corresponding to the current batch $B_t$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
\mathcal{L}(B_t)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}_{w}\bigl(W_s z_s(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{SarcTrain},\\[9pt]
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}\bigl(W_a z_a(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{AuthTrain},
\end{cases}$}
\end{equation}

where $\mathrm{CE}_w$ is a pre-specified cost-sensitive cross-entropy for sarcasm (weights are provided in \Appref{app:hyper}); because SarcTrain is class-balanced, the weighting emphasizes errors on sarcastic examples rather than correcting training-set imbalance. Softmax is applied to logits only when probabilities are required. The gradient flow follows from the equation: on sarcasm batches, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_a = 0$ (symmetrically, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_s = 0$ on MGTD batches), whereas $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ receives gradients from both types of batches. Unless otherwise stated, binary predictions use a fixed probability threshold of 0.5 rather than a threshold tuned on DualTest.

The encoder uses a lower learning rate than newly added modules. The sarcasm-head rate is fixed across configurations; MGTD-head rates are reported in \Appref{app:hyper}.

Model selection accounts for both tasks. At epoch $e$, the early-stopping signal is the equally weighted sum of the two validation losses, each normalized by its first-epoch value:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stop}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}^{(e)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(1)}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(1)}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$ are the epoch-$e$ validation losses for the sarcasm and MGTD tasks on their respective validation sets. Normalization reduces the influence of differences in task-specific loss scales and learning dynamics.

Five runs are defined by the SarcTrain validation folds: four folds train and one validates sarcasm, while AuthTrain uses a fixed 90/10 split. Each run reloads the pretrained encoder and randomly initializes new modules and heads. Configurations share fold assignments and nominal seeds, enabling split-matched comparisons but not identical initialization.

DualTest enters no training or validation split for the sarcasm or MGTD models. Five-run standard deviations combine fold and initialization variation. Wilson intervals are conditional on a fixed final model and cover test-instance, not training or generator, uncertainty (\Tabref{tab:quadrant}; \hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}).

Each subgroup configuration has a final model trained on all applicable data with a fixed epoch count determined from the five validation runs, an independent seed, and no early stopping. Their weights and training duration are not selected using DualTest, and they are not used in five-run aggregates; \Sref{sec:eval} uses the final RCNN dual-head model (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\section{Experiments and Diagnosis}
\label{sec:exp}

With the sarcasm and MGTD models fixed, we use DualTest first to compare encoder backbones and intermediate configurations, then to measure the change between the evaluated joint- and single-task procedures, and finally to partition predictions into source $\times$ sarcasm groups. Aggregate results are five-run means $\pm$ standard deviations; fixed final models support paired instance-level subgroup comparisons and Wilson intervals.

\subsection{Backbone Comparison}
\label{sec:backbone}

We compare dual-head mBERT \citep{devlin2019bert}, XLM-RoBERTa-base \citep{conneau2020xlmr}, DeBERTa-v3-base, and DeBERTa-v3-large \citep{he2023debertav3} under one evaluation protocol, adding the two intermediate variants on the large encoder. Positive-class F1 uses the sarcastic class for sarcasm and the human class for MGTD on full DualTest.

\Tabref{tab:backbone} shows higher mean F1 for the evaluated DeBERTa configurations than for mBERT and XLM-R. RCNN has the highest means, reaching 0.6309 sarcasm F1 and 0.9301 MGTD F1. Across the five runs, the two intermediate variants also have 34--47\% lower observed standard deviations than the plain large model, providing configuration-level evidence of lower run-to-run variation. The comparison evaluates complete configurations that jointly differ in backbone identity, scale, and intermediate representation. For the separately trained final RCNN model, sarcasm F1 is 0.819 in Chinese and 0.429 in English; prevalence, genre, annotation, and language all contribute to this difference, so it should not be read as a pure language effect. \Appref{app:results} provides macro-averaged, human-subset, and language-stratified results.

\subsection{Source $\times$ Sarcasm Subgroup Analysis}
\label{sec:quadrant}

An overall F1 score can hide a failure concentrated in one type of text. We therefore divide DualTest into four groups defined by source and sarcasm label. Human labels are dataset annotations, whereas AI labels record the prompted condition. Because each group contains only one reference class, we report recall for sarcastic texts and false-positive rate for non-sarcastic texts.

Their difference therefore measures the complete joint-training procedure. \Tabref{tab:quadrant} reports sarcasm predictions; corresponding MGTD results are in \Appref{app:results}.

The plain dual-head model predicts sarcasm for 86.3\% of AI $\times$ non-sarcastic instances. The observed rates are 40.9\% for interaction projection and 34.1\% for RCNN, while both same-architecture single-head controls yield 19.3\%. Thus, the observed severity is more than 45 pp lower in the two evaluated intermediate configurations than in the plain dual-head configuration.

Among the same AI non-sarcastic texts, 1{,}021 are false positives only for the plain dual-head model, whereas 7 are false positives only for its single-head control ($p<.001$). The control receives the same all-human sarcasm supervision but not the joint MGTD procedure, tying the large increase to the evaluated joint-training procedure. RCNN shows the same direction with a smaller gap. The matched contrasts are provided by the plain and RCNN models, while interaction projection adds complementary configuration-level evidence.

No analogous extreme subgroup error appears in the MGTD head: its false-positive rate is 6.1\% for plain and 6.6\% for RCNN. Within AI-generated texts, sarcasm AUC remains 0.82--0.88 across models, so the relative ranking of sarcastic and non-sarcastic AI texts is largely preserved. Joint training instead shifts predicted sarcasm probabilities upward, causing many texts prompted to be non-sarcastic to cross the fixed 0.5 threshold. The observed failure is therefore an AI-associated score increase on this subgroup, not a complete loss of within-AI ranking ability (\hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}).

The matched plain comparison makes the concentration especially clear: its non-sarcastic false-positive rate changes by 4.2 pp on human text but by 67.0 pp on AI-generated text. The aggregate F1 decrease is therefore driven by a sharply localized source $\times$ class effect rather than a uniform loss across DualTest.

\subsubsection{Mechanism Hypothesis and Representation-Level Diagnostics}
\label{sec:mechanism}

We next ask whether source information in the shared representation is connected to the higher sarcasm scores. A linear probe predicts source from both dual-head (AUC 0.986) and same-architecture single-head (0.974) representations. The comparable single-head AUC establishes source decodability as a background property shared by both models. In the dual-head model, texts with a higher MGTD-head AI logit also tend to have a higher sarcasm logit in the target subgroup ($\rho=0.519$). After covariance normalization, the source directions estimated from the MGTD head and the probe are also moderately aligned with the sarcasm direction. Together, the logit correlation and directional alignment provide correlational evidence linking source-related structure to the affected sarcasm decision.

We then modify the frozen representations and recompute predictions. LEACE \citep{belrose2023leace}, which reduces linearly decodable source information, lowers source-probe AUC from 0.982 to 0.589. It also changes the AI/human non-sarcastic false-positive rates from 84.8\%/32.8\% to 68.7\%/49.5\%. The nearly unchanged unweighted mean (58.8\% to 59.1\%) shows that LEACE specifically reallocates errors between source groups, revealing that the affected directions shape source-conditioned error allocation.

A key control removes a high-variance direction constructed to be orthogonal to the estimated linear source direction. This changes the AI/human false-positive rates to 66.7\%/54.1\% even though measured source decodability remains 0.982; ten sampled random directions show no comparable change. Together, these controls show prediction sensitivity to a non-random high-variance direction. The source-correlated shortcut hypothesis therefore provides a focused mechanism for further analysis, with the semantic content of the effective direction as the next causal target (\Appref{app:repr}).

\subsubsection{Cross-Generator Replication}
\label{sec:crossgen}

We keep the trained detectors fixed and compare the DualTest AI texts generated by DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B with replacements generated by Qwen3.5-9B (Ollama tag \texttt{qwen3.5:9b}) \citep{qwen2026qwen35}. After quality control, the DeepSeek-generated and Qwen3.5-generated non-sarcastic subsets contain 1{,}513 and 1{,}550 outputs. The plain dual-head false-positive rates are 86.3\% and 91.3\%, whereas its single-head control gives 19.3\% and 7.9\%; the dual--single gap therefore changes from 67.0 to 83.4 pp. On the Qwen3.5-generated subset, RCNN and interaction projection remain much lower than the plain dual model at 22.1\% and 29.5\%. The same gap direction and configuration ordering appear with Qwen3.5, although the magnitudes differ (\hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}).

\section{Exploratory Comparison of Detector Scores and LLM-Judge Ratings}
\label{sec:eval}

As a downstream implication, we test whether source-classification probability tracks perceived human-likeness and whether sarcasm probability tracks LLM ratings. Human-authorship probability and perceived human-likeness are distinct constructs; their comparison directly tests whether a classifier score can function as a generation-evaluation proxy. We compare 600 outputs from Qwen3-8B \citep{qwen2025} before and after QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023qlora} fine-tuning on GenTrain. The RCNN detector and three LLM judges \citep{deepseek2024v3,gemini2025report,openai2026gpt5} score outputs from 180 Chinese topic-comment and 120 English rewriting prompts; the judges are not shown the ground-truth source, and language is coupled with generation task.

The detector probabilities and judge ratings do not move together consistently. In Chinese, SFT raises the RCNN detector's sarcasm score from 0.780 to 0.895 but lowers the mean LLM sarcasm rating from 3.99 to 3.80; the sarcasm correlation is nearly zero in Chinese ($-0.020$) but positive in English ($+0.338$). The clearest condition-level reversal concerns human-likeness: SFT raises human-authorship probabilities in both task--language settings while mean perceived human-likeness ratings decline, yielding a pooled correlation of $-0.212$. Within-condition correlations are weaker, showing that the pooled negative value is driven mainly by the reversal between conditions.

The sarcasm-detector change is setting-dependent: $+0.115$ in Chinese and $-0.124$ in English. By contrast, all six language $\times$ judge comparisons give SFT lower mean sarcasm ratings, five significantly after FDR correction. Detector probabilities can therefore remain useful classification outputs while failing to preserve the ordering or condition-level direction required of style or quality scores.

Agreement among the three judges is low to moderate at the individual-text level. A plain-detector control changes the reported correlations by at most 0.02. The stable human-authorship/human-likeness reversal and the judge-specific sarcasm results therefore motivate external validation before detector probabilities are reused as generation-quality scores or rewards. Full protocols, tests, and illustrative cases are reported in \hyperref[app:results]{Appendices~\ref*{app:results}}--\hyperref[app:cases]{\ref*{app:cases}}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

LLM-based augmentation requires generated examples to convey sarcasm without turning source cues into shortcuts. Our results expose this risk: compared with a same-architecture single-head control, the plain dual-head model predicts sarcasm for AI text prompted to be non-sarcastic, raising its false-positive rate by 67.0 pp. The direction holds across languages and when the AI test texts are regenerated with Qwen3.5-9B; RCNN and interaction projection show lower rates. Representation interventions show that source-related and other high-variance directions shape how errors are distributed across source groups, providing concrete targets for further causal analysis. Human-authorship probabilities diverge from perceived human-likeness, so detector scores require external, source $\times$ class-stratified validation before use as quality or reward signals.

\section*{Limitations}

DualTest combines human sarcasm annotations with instruction-conditioned labels for AI texts, so AI-side false positives are defined relative to requested style rather than independent perceived-sarcasm annotation. DeepSeek generation failures leave 80 human texts without generated counterparts. Some SarcTrain duplicates span validation folds and may affect checkpoint selection; these records do not enter DualTest.

The core dual--single comparisons identify the complete joint-training procedure: architecture, sarcasm data, folds, sarcasm-head learning rate, and class weights are shared, while MGTD batches, task scheduling, shared updates, and joint early stopping enter together. Compute-matched controls could further decompose this effect. Cross-architecture results are configuration-level because capacity and optimization differ, and the cost-sensitive loss can affect absolute rates. AuthTrain is generated with DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B; Qwen3.5-9B replaces only DualTest's AI side and therefore provides directional test-set replication rather than retraining with another generator.

The fixed-model linear analyses connect source-related structure to behavior but do not isolate a unique causal feature or training-seed distribution. The detector--judge study uses LLM rather than human ratings and couples language with generation task. These boundaries motivate independent style annotation, matched-budget controls, cross-seed mechanism tests, and broader generator and human-evaluation studies.

\section*{Ethical considerations}

All human-authored texts come from publicly released research datasets: iSarcasm, CSC, ToSarcasm, News Headlines, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus. Explicit repository licenses were available for iSarcasm and CSC; for sources without an identified redistribution license, we do not redistribute the human-authored text (\Appref{app:data}). We collect no new user data. We did not conduct an additional personally identifiable information or offensive-content audit beyond the preparation reflected in the released corpora.

All AI texts were generated locally using publicly released open-weight models (DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B, Qwen3.5-9B, and the base and QLoRA-adapted Qwen3-8B). They are used only for training and evaluation and are labeled as AI-generated. The study recruits no new human participants, but analyzes human-authored texts from previously released datasets. The automated evaluation transmits each evaluation input, generated output, and rating instructions to the DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, and GPT-5-nano APIs; it does not transmit dataset metadata or ground-truth source labels.

The detectors are developed for research diagnosis. Their outputs should not be used as evidence of authorship or misconduct at the individual-text level without human review and domain-specific validation, nor deployed for content moderation or automated sanctions without evaluating out-of-distribution behavior. The class-conditional failure diagnosed here is an example of systematic subgroup error affecting a particular type of generated content.

AI-assisted tools were used for grammar correction, wording suggestions, and formatting. They did not generate the reported experimental outputs or statistics; the authors determined the study design, analysis, and conclusions and take full responsibility for the work.
