\begin{abstract}
A deployed system may have to judge, for the same text, both whether it is sarcastic and whether a human wrote it, yet the two detection tasks are almost always studied apart. We train them jointly in a bilingual Chinese--English setting and build DualTest (4{,}664 instances), a diagnostic test set stratified by source and sarcasm label; labels on the AI side reflect the requested generation style rather than independent human annotation. Joint training turns out to have a sharply localized cost. Relative to a single-head sarcasm model with the same supervision, adding an authorship head raises the false-positive rate on AI-generated non-sarcastic text from 19.3\% to 86.3\%. The failure replicates with a second generator (91.3\%), and two intermediate architectural modules reduce it substantially. Probing shows that source information is decodable and associated with sarcasm decisions; a control that leaves source information intact, however, reproduces the same effect, so we characterize the failure as a source-correlated, style-entangled shortcut, with the source concept's specific causal role remaining a hypothesis. Detector scores also diverge from blinded LLM-judge evaluations---one more reason not to use authorship detectors directly as evaluation or reward signals for sarcasm generation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A sarcastic sentence usually means something other than what it literally says, and often the opposite. With few stable lexical cues to anchor on, sarcasm detection has long depended on context, intent, and background knowledge; annotation agreement is relatively low, and multilingual settings are harder still. LLMs bring a further complication. Sarcastic social-media text may now be written by a person or generated by a model under instruction, so a deployed system can face ``is this sarcastic?'' and ``was this written by a human?'' on the same input at the same time. The generation side couples the two questions as well: detector scores are already used in practice as evaluation and even reward signals for sarcasm generation (e.g., ViSP; \citealp{wang2026visp}; \Sref{sec:related-eval}), and judging whether generated sarcasm reads as human-written involves both dimensions at once. Existing work nonetheless treats sentiment/rhetorical analysis and content provenance/safety detection separately, and to our knowledge no prior work unifies sarcasm detection and AI-authorship detection as a joint multi-task learning problem (\Sref{sec:related-ai}).

What can go wrong when these two judgments share a representation? When a single textual backbone supports both source identification and semantic judgment, the representation shaped by one task may alter how the other behaves on particular subgroups, whatever the architecture. We work with a dual-head model because it keeps this risk observable and localizable at minimal structural cost. Jointly trained with the authorship task, the sarcasm head systematically misclassifies AI-generated non-sarcastic text as sarcastic---a form of class-conditional negative transfer. The error is induced by joint training; it persists when the AI test texts are regenerated by a second generator, and intermediate modules mitigate it. At the mechanism level, probing shows that source information is highly decodable from the shared representation, correlated with sarcasm decisions, and aligned with the decision direction in whitened geometry. Concept-erasure experiments, however, locate the effect in a dominant-variance subspace entangled with stylistic factors (\Sref{sec:mechanism}, \Appref{app:repr}). The claim that the sarcasm head exploits a source-correlated shortcut is thus a mechanism hypothesis with preliminary support; confirming it will require causal mediation and nonlinear tools, which we leave to dedicated study. An orthogonal control makes concrete the gap between a decodable, decision-correlated signal and an established mechanism, in line with methodological critiques of probing \citep{hewitt2019probes,elazar2021amnesic}.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We formulate joint Chinese--English sarcasm and AI-authorship detection and introduce DualTest, which supports subgroup analysis across four source $\times$ sarcasm quadrants; this is the first study, to our knowledge, to examine the two tasks jointly at this granularity. Controlled single-head, dual-head, architectural, and cross-generator comparisons then reveal a class-conditional negative-transfer failure concentrated on AI-generated non-sarcastic text. Finally, representation analyses characterize the failure as source-correlated and style-entangled, and a detector--judge comparison shows that detector scores may be unreliable as standalone generation-evaluation signals.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Sarcasm Detection and Multilingual Resources}
\label{sec:related-sarcasm}

Feature engineering \citep{riloff2013sarcasm,ghosh2016fracking}, neural sequence models \citep{tay2018reasoning}, and pretrained language models \citep{khatri2020,du2022pali} have each pushed sarcasm detection forward, but the task stays hard in multilingual, intent-oriented, class-imbalanced settings: the best English systems on iSarcasmEval \citep{abufarha2022isarcasmeval} reach sarcasm F1 of roughly 0.56--0.61, zero-shot LLMs on MultiPICo \citep{casola2024multipico} 0.15--0.57, and SarcNet \citep{yue2024sarcnet} 0.73. \citet{yang2024influence}, building on their earlier multilingual mBERT/BiLSTM study \citep{yang2023impact}, introduced mBERT-RCNN (mBERT with the recurrent convolutional text-classification architecture of \citealp{lai2015rcnn}) and ChatGPT data augmentation, finding that the RCNN's local feature extraction improved robustness on mixed datasets of typologically distinct languages, an architectural line this work follows (\Sref{sec:model-arch}). Because those results come from different test sets, \Sref{sec:backbone} provides directly comparable mBERT dual-head baselines on our test set. Our setting is parallel Chinese--English; what existing resources lack, and what DualTest supplies, is evaluation data labeled jointly for sarcasm and AI authorship.

\subsection{AI-Generated Text Detection and Authorship Verification}
\label{sec:related-ai}

AI-text detection uses zero-shot/perplexity-based detectors \citep{mitchell2023detectgpt} and discriminative models trained on paired human--machine corpora \citep[HC3;][]{guo2023hc3}; generalization is the main difficulty; M4 \citep{wang2024m4} targets black-box detection across generators, domains, and languages, and RAID \citep{dugan2024raid} stress-tests detector robustness. Conceptually, \citet{bevendorff2025} frame the problem as authorship verification rather than quality assessment; we treat the authorship judgment as an auxiliary task within a shared encoder. Existing multi-task sarcasm research mainly combines sarcasm with sentiment, stance, or emotion. We found no prior study that jointly trains sarcasm and AI-authorship prediction under a shared encoder;\footnote{Searched in May 2026 across Semantic Scholar, arXiv, ACL Anthology, and Google Scholar, using keyword combinations including ``sarcasm detection,'' ``AI-generated text detection,'' ``authorship detection,'' ``multi-task learning,'' and ``joint detection.''} that gap is the starting point of this study.

\subsection{Multi-Task Negative Transfer, Shortcut Learning, and Shared-Encoder Mitigation}
\label{sec:related-transfer}

In multi-task learning, auxiliary tasks are not always beneficial: transfer direction depends on task relatedness and data geometry \citep{zhang2023survey,wu2020understanding}, and conflicting gradients can cause negative transfer \citep{yu2020gradientsurgery,liu2021cagrad}. \citet{mueller2022taskconflict} provide the closest NLP precedent, showing through single-/multi-task contrasts that models may infer task identity from the input distribution and destabilize on out-of-distribution inputs \citep[see also][on internal mechanisms of multi-task models]{he2021stemcell,li2023functional}. Shortcut learning offers a complementary reading: models may exploit features that are predictive in the training distribution yet fail on specific subgroups \citep{geirhos2020shortcut,ye2024spurious}; in multi-task settings this shows up as increased absorption of spurious correlations \citep{hu2022spurious,chai2025spurious} and as subgroup-stratified failures \citep{compton2023morisless}. Mitigation under a shared encoder takes two broad forms: some methods isolate task-specific parameters (AdapterFusion, \citealp{pfeiffer2021adapterfusion}; MAD-X, \citealp{pfeiffer2020madx}; HyperFormer++, \citealp{mahabadi2021hyperformer}; PALs, \citealp{stickland2019pals}), while others filter interference inside the shared representation through gating, attention, or intermediate modules (\citealp{wu2019sifted}; CA-MTL, \citealp{pilault2021camtl}); our intermediate-layer analysis belongs with the second group. In this paper, conditional stratification exposes a subgroup-specific failure induced by joint training, and controlled comparisons of three intermediate structures test the mitigation hypothesis (\Sref{sec:quadrant}).

\subsection{Misalignment in Detector-Based Evaluation and Rewards}
\label{sec:related-eval}

Automated scorers are increasingly used as evaluation or reward signals for generation \citep{zheng2023judging}; optimization can then end up targeting a model-specific decision boundary rather than perceived quality. Sarcasm generation already runs this risk. ViSP \citep{wang2026visp} uses the DIP sarcasm detector \citep{wen2023dip} as a PPO reward \citep{schulman2017ppo} and acknowledges dependence on evaluator quality. \hyperref[sec:eval]{Section~\ref*{sec:eval}} tests whether our detector scores agree with independent blinded LLM judgments.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:data}

We begin with five heterogeneous sources (iSarcasm: \citealp{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval}; CSC: \citealp{jang2024csc,jang2023intended}; ToSarcasm: \citealp{liang2022tosarcasm}; News Headlines v2: \citealp{misra2023headlines,misra2021sculpting}; Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus: \citealp{zhu2022corpus}; 44{,}862 instances after standardized merging, approximately 87\% English) and obtain three training sets and one diagnostic test set through a three-stage pipeline. Source-level details, annotation methods, and quality checks are provided in \Appref{app:data}.

\paragraph{Sarcasm classification training set, SarcTrain (10{,}000, all human).} The dataset is fully balanced across four language $\times$ label buckets, with 2{,}500 instances per bucket through downsampling, and is pre-split into five folds. All texts in this set are human-authored.

\paragraph{Authorship classification training set, AuthTrain (19{,}995).} The human side is sampled directly from SarcTrain with language balance. On the AI side, one same-topic version is generated for each human instance using deepseek-r1:8b \citep[via Ollama]{deepseek2025r1}: paired AI versions were successfully obtained for 9{,}996 of 9{,}999 human instances, while three generation failures were retained only on the human side. Generation instructions are selected from four templates (Chinese/English $\times$ sarcastic/normal) according to the seed instance's sarcasm label, so that the AI texts likewise cover both sarcastic and non-sarcastic styles and the authorship label is independent of sarcastic style. Generation parameters, full templates, and post-processing steps are given in \Appref{app:data}.

\paragraph{Generation training set, GenTrain (2{,}428; used in \Sref{sec:eval}).} This set is drawn from two sources containing topic or contextual information (iSarcasm and ToSarcasm; see \Appref{app:data} for their uses). Only positive sarcasm instances are retained; approximately 40\% are sampled, and the minority language is oversampled to 1{,}214 instances in each of Chinese and English. By prompt type, the set contains 374 rewriting, 839 response, and 1{,}215 comment instances, and serves as the instruction-tuning data for SFT.

\paragraph{Diagnostic test set, DualTest (4{,}664).} DualTest combines the official iSarcasm test split (1{,}400 English instances) and the ToSarcasm test set (972 Chinese instances) with AI-generated counterparts produced by deepseek-r1:8b. Human instances retain their original sarcasm annotations. For AI instances, \texttt{is\_human} is false, while the sarcasm label denotes the style requested by the corresponding ``sarcastic style'' or ``normal style'' generation instruction (full templates in \Appref{app:data}). For this reason we treat the AI-side labels as instruction-conditioned diagnostic labels rather than independently annotated judgments of perceived sarcasm. The four source $\times$ sarcasm groups contain 823 human-sarcastic, 1{,}549 human-non-sarcastic, 779 AI-sarcastic, and 1{,}513 AI-non-sarcastic instances (see \Appref{app:data} for the three-dimensional distribution). The dataset preserves the natural composition of the original test splits.

\section{Dual-Head Detection Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Tasks and Architecture}
\label{sec:model-arch}

Given a Chinese or English input text $x$, the model simultaneously produces two binary judgments: whether $x$ conveys sarcastic intent and whether $x$ was written by a human. The two tasks share a single DeBERTa-v3-large encoder \citep[this encoder was pretrained on English corpora, and \Sref{sec:backbone} directly compares it with multilingual-pretrained mBERT / XLM-R on the same bilingual test set]{he2023debertav3}. Encoder outputs are aggregated by an intermediate module and then passed to two independent classification heads; \Figref{fig:architecture} shows the main model.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_architecture.pdf}
  \caption{Main dual-head model: a shared DeBERTa-v3-large encoder, an RCNN intermediate module (dashed), and two task-specific heads. The controlled comparisons in \Sref{sec:exp} vary only the dashed module: it is removed in the plain dual-head and replaced by task-branch interaction projection in the variant (\Appref{app:hyper}).}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The main model uses BiLSTM $+$ CNN (RCNN) as its intermediate module: the encoder output sequence passes through a single-layer bidirectional BiLSTM (hidden size 256), is concatenated with the original sequence, and is aggregated into a 384-dimensional vector $z$ by three groups of one-dimensional convolutions (kernel widths 3/4/5, 128 channels each) with global max pooling; after dropout, $z$ is passed to the two heads. This intermediate module follows the mBERT-RCNN architectural idea of \citet{yang2024influence}. The controlled comparisons in \Sref{sec:exp} include two additional variants. The plain dual-head model removes the intermediate module and passes the \texttt{[CLS]} representation directly to both heads. The task-branch interaction projection model replaces RCNN with two 256-dimensional task-branch projections of the \texttt{[CLS]} representation, $h_s$ and $h_a$, each augmented by a residual attention read of the opposite branch: $h_s' = h_s + \mathrm{Attn}(Q{=}h_s, K{=}h_a, V{=}h_a)$, and symmetrically for $h_a'$. Because each branch consists of a single vector, the attention weight is identically 1 and the module reduces to a residual addition of a linear projection from the opposite branch; we name the variant by this actual computation rather than as a form of cross-attention (hereafter, the interaction-projection dual-head model). The encoder and classification heads are identical across all three models; only the intermediate structure differs, which is what makes the comparison of the intermediate layer's role a controlled one. Remaining dimensions and dropout settings are in \Appref{app:hyper}.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:model-train}

Training uses a mixed loader that alternates batches by task: each batch contains samples from only one task, and authorship and sarcasm batches alternate in a 2:1 ratio, matching the data-size ratio between AuthTrain and SarcTrain. Let the shared parameters be $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ (the encoder and intermediate module), let $z(x)$ be their aggregated representation for input $x$, and let $W_s$ and $W_a$ denote the two classification heads. At step $t$, the loss is computed only for the head corresponding to the task of the current batch $B_t$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
\mathcal{L}(B_t)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}_{w}\bigl(\sigma(W_s z(x)), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{SarcTrain},\\[9pt]
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}\bigl(\sigma(W_a z(x)), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{AuthTrain},
\end{cases}$}
\end{equation}

where $\sigma$ denotes softmax and $\mathrm{CE}_w$ is class-weighted cross-entropy that compensates for the scarcity of sarcastic positives (weights are provided in \Appref{app:hyper}). The gradient flow follows from the equation: on sarcasm batches, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_a = 0$ (symmetrically, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_s = 0$ on authorship batches), whereas $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ receives gradients from both types of batches.

Optimization uses separate learning rates for parameter groups: the pretrained encoder is updated at a much lower rate than the intermediate module and classification heads to preserve pretrained representations. Model selection accounts for both tasks: the early-stopping signal at epoch $e$ is the equally weighted sum of each task's validation loss normalized by its first-epoch value:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stop}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}^{(e)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(1)}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(1)}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$ are the epoch-$e$ validation losses for the sarcasm and authorship tasks on their respective validation sets. Normalization prevents early stopping from being dominated by the larger authorship task.

Training and evaluation use five fold-based runs with a fixed external test set. SarcTrain is pre-split into five folds: run $k$ trains the sarcasm task on four folds, with the held-out fold as the sarcasm validation set providing $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$; AuthTrain uses a fixed 90\% training / 10\% validation split shared by all runs, its validation portion providing $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$, so between-run variation is concentrated in changes to the sarcasm training data. Each run is trained from a fresh initialization of the same pretrained base; all architectures and ablations share the fold assignments and seed scheme, and cross-model comparisons are paired by split. The five models are then evaluated on both tasks on the external test set DualTest, which is involved in no split. The main tables report the mean $\pm$ standard deviation across the five runs (hereafter, five-run mean $\pm$ standard deviation); this standard deviation reflects between-run variation from training splits and initialization, while test-set sampling uncertainty is quantified separately through confidence intervals for the final model (\Tabref{tab:quadrant} and \hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}).

A separate final model is retrained on all of SarcTrain and AuthTrain using the same hyperparameters and a fixed epoch count determined from the five validation runs. It is used only for subgroup inference and \Sref{sec:eval} rescoring and is not selected on DualTest. It does not enter the five-run aggregate comparisons in \Tabref{tab:ablation} and \Tabref{tab:backbone}; its quadrant estimates are reported separately in \Tabref{tab:quadrant}. Class weights, sampling ratio, grouped learning rates, and full hyperparameters are provided in \Appref{app:hyper}.

\section{Experiments and Diagnosis}
\label{sec:exp}

The experiments compare encoder backbones, joint versus single-task training, and intermediate-layer architectural variants on overall F1, then use DualTest's dual labels to partition the same evaluation into four ``source $\times$ sarcasm'' quadrants, where a class-conditional failure emerges that overall F1 does not show. Aggregate model comparisons report the mean $\pm$ standard deviation over five fold-based runs. The quadrant analysis uses the separately trained final model to obtain one prediction per instance for paired subgroup tests; Wilson intervals quantify test-set uncertainty. The final model is not selected on DualTest.

\subsection{Backbone Comparison}
\label{sec:backbone}

We compare dual-head models across four encoder backbones (mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa-base, and DeBERTa-v3 base and large) and, on the large encoder, the two intermediate-module variants of \Sref{sec:model-arch}. The encoders span multilingual and English-centric pretraining: mBERT is BERT-base pretrained on multilingual Wikipedia \citep{devlin2019bert}, XLM-RoBERTa-base is pretrained on CommonCrawl text covering about one hundred languages \citep{conneau2020xlmr}, and DeBERTa-v3 combines disentangled attention with ELECTRA-style pretraining \citep{clark2020electra} on primarily English corpora. ``Sarcasm F1'' is the F1 of the sarcastic class (positive class), following the official iSarcasmEval convention, and ``Authorship F1'' is the F1 of the human class (positive class); both are computed over the full DualTest set, aggregating Chinese and English and both source sides, with AI-side sarcasm labels constructed from the generation instructions (\Sref{sec:data}).

\Tabref{tab:backbone} reports the comparison (five-run mean $\pm$ standard deviation); the main model reaches sarcasm F1 0.6309 $\pm$ 0.020 and authorship F1 0.9301 $\pm$ 0.005. Under the parameter scales and hyperparameter settings used here, DeBERTa-family encoders clearly outperform mBERT and XLM-R; the comparison mixes differences in parameter scale and pretraining corpora, and we use it only to pick the main model for the diagnosis. The two intermediate-layer variants differ little from the plain dual-head model in their means (with the main RCNN model performing better), but reduce between-run variance by approximately 34--47\%. Chinese sarcasm F1 is substantially higher than English sarcasm F1 (0.819 vs.\ 0.429 for the main model), primarily because of differences in sarcasm prevalence between the two language subsets (63.8\% vs.\ 14.2\%) and differences in source-benchmark difficulty (\hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}). Sarcasm F1 is substantially lower than authorship F1 for all models, and its absolute level of about 0.63 lies within the range of the multilingual, intent-oriented benchmarks cited in \Sref{sec:related-sarcasm}. Macro-averaged, human-subset, and per-language results for the main model are in \Appref{app:results}.

\subsection{Four-Quadrant Diagnosis: Class-Conditional Negative Transfer and Intermediate-Layer Buffering}
\label{sec:quadrant}

Aggregate metrics obscure the structure of errors. The overall sarcasm F1 of the plain dual-head model alone would read as unremarkable mid-range performance; in fact its errors concentrate on the AI-text subset. To localize this class-conditional failure, we partition DualTest by the ground-truth \texttt{is\_human} $\times$ \texttt{is\_sarcastic} labels into four quadrant subsets and examine the detector's sarcasm predictions separately within each. Because each quadrant contains only one ground-truth class, F1 degenerates on such subsets (precision is always 1 on positive-class subsets, while negative-class subsets contain no positive instances). We therefore report recall for positive-class subsets and the false-positive rate for negative-class subsets.

The single-task control and cross-generator replication establish the behavioral conclusion---class-conditional negative transfer exists and is induced by joint training---while representation-level tests supply correlational evidence for the shortcut hypothesis and locate the signal within the dominant-variance structure of the representation.

\subsubsection{Three Architectures and Single-Head Controls}
\label{sec:quadrant-results}

We compare the sarcasm predictions of five detectors on the same DualTest set. The three dual-head variants (plain / interaction-projection / RCNN) characterize how the severity of the failure changes with intermediate structure. The two single-head models share the same architecture and sarcasm supervision data (SarcTrain) as their corresponding dual-head models. They differ only in whether the auxiliary authorship task is jointly trained (that task's training objective and the AuthTrain data are added as a whole). The single-head model supplies the counterfactual: the same sarcasm classifier without joint training. Were the failure rooted in the sarcasm training data (SarcTrain contains only human text), the single-head model would fail in the same way; a failure induced by joint training, by contrast, should leave it untouched. \Tabref{tab:quadrant} reports sarcasm predictions. The failure is concentrated in the sarcasm head; the authorship head remains stable under the same partition (reported below and in \Appref{app:results}).

The plain dual-head model fails severely in the AI $\times$ non-sarcastic quadrant: it misclassifies 86.3\% of the instances as sarcastic, indicating that on this subset the model likely relies on cues associated with AI source rather than stable sarcastic semantics. Both structurally distinct intermediate modules substantially mitigate the failure, reducing it by more than 45 pp from 86.3\%, with mutually non-overlapping confidence intervals across the rates (\Tabref{tab:quadrant}). The approximately 7 pp gap between RCNN and interaction projection is smaller but remains significant (\hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}). The single-head models settle the counterfactual: despite also being trained only on human text, they have a false-positive rate of just 19.3\%. The difference from the plain dual-head model on the same texts is significant by a paired McNemar test (discordant pairs 1{,}021 / 7, $p < 0.001$; exact $p$ values and the remaining pairs are reported in \hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}). This rules out missing AI text in the sarcasm training data as an explanation: the failure is induced by joint training. The RCNN and plain single-head models also behave almost identically (both approximately 19.3\%): RCNN's mitigating effect emerges only in the presence of joint training, not from inherent architectural robustness to AI text.

The authorship head provides a control for the failure's directionality. On the same AI $\times$ non-sarcastic subset, its false-positive rate is only 6.1\% (plain) / 6.6\% (RCNN), and it remains stable across all four quadrants; the single-head authorship counterfactual performs at the same level as the dual-head models (complete values in \Appref{app:results}). On the same set of AI texts, the problem is concentrated in the sarcasm head and the gap is on the order of tens of percentage points. The failure is jointly conditioned on source and class and is most extreme on the AI $\times$ non-sarcastic subset, hence the term ``class-conditional negative transfer.'' This subset-level failure and the asymmetric aggregate transfer in \Sref{sec:ablation} are two views of the same negative transfer.

\subsubsection{Mechanism Explanation and Levels of Evidence}
\label{sec:mechanism}

Because authorship gradients update the shared representation whereas SarcTrain contains only human texts, we hypothesize that the sarcasm head exploits source-correlated style cues that are not corrected by sarcasm supervision on AI text. Under this interpretation, an intermediate module may partially buffer changes in the shared representation. This account is a hypothesis rather than an established causal mechanism.

The representation-level evidence is mixed: source is highly decodable and correlated with sarcasm decisions, and LEACE \citep{belrose2023leace} reduces both source decodability and the AI $\times$ non-sarcastic false-positive rate. However, removing a high-variance direction orthogonal to source produces a comparable reduction while leaving source decodability intact, whereas ten random-direction placebos leave the rate unchanged. The effective interventions thus pick out a dominant-variance, style-entangled subspace but do not establish source identity as the causal feature, which is why we stay with the term source-correlated shortcut. Full probing, erasure, and control results are reported in \Appref{app:repr}.

\subsubsection{Cross-Generator Replication}
\label{sec:crossgen}

We regenerate the AI side of DualTest with qwen3.5:9b \citep{qwen2026qwen35} while keeping the human side and trained detectors unchanged; \Figref{fig:quadrant} plots the five detectors' rates on both generators with Wilson 95\% confidence intervals, and \hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7} tabulates them with paired tests. The plain dual-head false-positive rate on AI-generated non-sarcastic text increases from 86.3\% to 91.3\%, whereas the corresponding single-head rate decreases from 19.3\% to 7.9\%. Mitigation is also preserved for RCNN (34.1\% $\to$ 22.1\%) and interaction projection (40.9\% $\to$ 29.5\%). The second generator thus reproduces the class-conditional failure; we have not tested broader generator families.

\section{Detector--Judge Misalignment}
\label{sec:eval}

To test whether detector scores track an independent generation-evaluation signal, we evaluate 600 base and SFT outputs from 300 prompts using the main-model detector and three blinded LLM judges (DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, and GPT-5-nano; \citealp{deepseek2024v3,gemini2025report,openai2026gpt5}). The SFT model is Qwen3-8B \citep{qwen2025} fine-tuned on GenTrain with QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023qlora}; the protocol is detailed in \hyperref[app:hyper]{Appendices~\ref*{app:hyper}}--\hyperref[app:results]{\ref*{app:results}}. Their rankings diverge: in Chinese, SFT raises the detector's sarcasm score from 0.780 to 0.895 while reducing the mean judge score from 4.01 to 3.71. The detector also rates SFT outputs as more human-like in both languages, whereas the judges show the opposite direction. Pooled Spearman correlation is $-0.212$ for human-likeness and $-0.020$ for Chinese sarcasm.

The judges are LLMs, not human annotators, so what these results establish is detector--judge misalignment; how human readers would rate the same outputs is a question we do not answer. Even so, detector scores and an independent evaluation signal can rank the same outputs differently, and that alone argues against using detector scores as standalone generation rewards. The full protocol, per-judge tests, within-condition correlations, language analysis, and representative cases are reported in \hyperref[app:results]{Appendices~\ref*{app:results}}--\hyperref[app:cases]{\ref*{app:cases}}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Training sarcasm and AI-authorship detection together can fail in a way that overall metrics hide. The plain dual-head model, matched in every other respect to its single-head counterpart, sharply overpredicts sarcasm on AI-generated non-sarcastic text; the failure persists with a second generator, and two intermediate modules reduce it. Representation analyses give the failure a name and a location---a source-correlated, style-entangled shortcut in a dominant-variance subspace---with causal attribution still open. On the generation side, SFT produced a language asymmetry, and detector and judge scores ranked the same outputs differently, so neither authorship nor sarcasm detector scores should be treated as standalone generation-quality signals. Whenever related judgments are learned through one shared textual representation, evaluation should be stratified by subgroup.

\section*{Limitations}

DualTest combines human-authored texts with original sarcasm annotations and AI-generated texts with instruction-conditioned style labels. The AI-side sarcasm labels were not independently annotated by humans, so absolute scores on that side measure performance relative to the requested generation style. Replication with a second generator shows that the observed failure is not limited to deepseek-r1:8b, but does not establish generalization across broader open- and closed-source generator families. The source datasets also differ in their operational definitions of sarcasm, and the model does not use conversational context.

The mechanism evidence is primarily behavioral and linear. Source information is decodable and correlated with sarcasm decisions, yet the orthogonal high-variance control blocks any causal attribution to source identity. The buffering account of the intermediate modules is, for now, only descriptive: we have neither capacity-matched null modules nor a direct gradient analysis.

Finally, the generation analysis relies on three blinded LLM judges rather than human evaluators, with a 60:40 Chinese--English sample ratio; it supports a directional disagreement between detector and judge scores, not a claim about human-perceived generation quality or its precise language-specific magnitude. We also stopped short of reward-based generation. Folding the observed misalignment into reward design and measuring its dynamic effects under reinforcement learning is future work; so are causal-mediation and nonlinear tests for source attribution, and parameter-isolating or data-side mitigations of the failure.

\section*{Ethical considerations}

All human-authored texts used in this study come from publicly released research datasets (iSarcasm, CSC, ToSarcasm, News Headlines, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus) and are used in accordance with their original licenses. We collect no new user data and process no personally identifiable information. All AI texts were generated locally by open-source models (deepseek-r1:8b and qwen3.5:9b); they appear only in training and evaluation and are always labeled as machine-generated. The blinded LLM evaluation (DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, and GPT-5-nano) consists of automated model-API calls and involves no human participants. The detectors are developed for research diagnosis. We explicitly warn (\Sref{sec:eval}) that their scores should not be used directly as generation-quality or reward signals, nor should the detectors be deployed in high-risk settings such as content moderation without evaluating out-of-distribution behavior. The class-conditional failure diagnosed here (the systematic misclassification of AI-generated non-sarcastic text) is itself an example of the systematic bias such systems can direct at particular content subgroups. AI-assisted tools were used during writing for language polishing and formatting; the study design, experiments, and conclusions are the authors' own, and the authors take full responsibility for the work.
