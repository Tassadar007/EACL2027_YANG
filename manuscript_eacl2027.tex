\begin{abstract}
Sarcasm detection requires intent beyond literal wording. Large language models (LLMs) can expand sarcasm resources, but evaluation must distinguish sarcasm-relevant pragmatic cues from source-related ones. A shared encoder supports both sarcasm detection and machine-generated text detection (MGTD), yet source-predictive cues may distort sarcasm predictions. We harmonize Chinese and English resources and construct DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup analysis, with prompt-specified style serving as the sarcasm label for AI-generated texts. On AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic, the final plain joint model's false-positive rate exceeds that of its sarcasm-only counterpart by 67.0 percentage points, a pattern we call \emph{class-conditional negative transfer}. Scores shift upward despite substantial within-AI discrimination. Both languages show the pattern, which persists when unchanged detectors evaluate a second test set generated by another LLM. Representation diagnostics link source-related scores and directions to the affected sarcasm decisions and show that removing one data-derived high-variance direction changes the subgroup gap. Exploratory analysis finds that predicted human-authorship probabilities do not consistently track LLM-judge ratings of human-likeness obtained without ground-truth source labels. These findings motivate source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup evaluation and external validation before detector probabilities are reused as quality scores or rewards.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sarcasm expresses an intended attitude that may differ from an utterance's literal meaning, so automatic detection depends on implicit contrast, communicative intent, and context. Errors can reverse sentiment predictions and disrupt opinion-mining or content-moderation systems \citep{tay2018reasoning,vidgen2019challenges}. Datasets also differ in how sarcastic texts are elicited or collected and whose judgments define the labels; these choices, together with domain, style, and language, affect annotation and model behavior \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,casola2024multipico,jang2024csc}.

Large language models (LLMs) offer a way to expand sarcasm resources, but their usefulness depends on realizing the requested style without turning source-specific regularities into shortcuts. Evaluation should therefore separate source and sarcasm: both human-authored and AI-generated texts may be sarcastic or non-sarcastic. We refer to the four resulting combinations---human-authored sarcastic, human-authored non-sarcastic, AI-generated sarcastic, and AI-generated non-sarcastic texts---as source $\times$ sarcasm subgroups.

A shared encoder with task-specific heads can reuse one representation for both predictions, but it also creates a route for interference: machine-generated text detection (MGTD) favors features that distinguish human-authored from AI-generated text \citep{guo2023hc3,wang2024m4}, whereas sarcasm detection should reflect intent rather than source. The labels themselves do not conflict; the risk lies in how the tasks organize the shared representation. If source-predictive cues influence the sarcasm head, information useful for MGTD but spurious for sarcasm can produce task conflict and negative transfer \citep{mueller2022taskconflict,hu2022spurious}. This motivates testing how joint training changes sarcasm predictions.

Because source-predictive regularities and sarcasm cues vary across languages and corpora \citep{jang2024csc,casola2024multipico,wang2024m4}, we examine the transfer pattern separately in Chinese and English.

In this paper, we construct DualTest, a bilingual evaluation set for studying joint MGTD--sarcasm training with separate source and sarcasm labels across these four subgroups. For AI-generated texts, the sarcasm label records prompt-specified style. Using DualTest and same-configuration sarcasm-only controls, we show that joint training yields sharply higher false-positive rates on AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic. We call this pattern \emph{class-conditional negative transfer} because the degradation is concentrated in one source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup rather than distributed uniformly across DualTest. The effect appears in both languages and persists when the same detectors evaluate outputs from another generator without retraining.

To connect decodable information with model behavior beyond probe accuracy, we use linear probes to test whether source can be recovered from a representation, compare MGTD- and sarcasm-head scores to test whether their decisions move together, and intervene on frozen representations by removing selected directions before reapplying the original sarcasm head \citep{hewitt2019probes,elazar2021amnesic}. Source-related scores and directions co-vary with the affected sarcasm decisions, and removing one data-derived high-variance direction changes the subgroup gap.

As a supplementary exploratory analysis, we examine whether detector probabilities agree with separately obtained ratings of sarcasm and human-likeness. This matters because classifier scores may be reused as evaluation or reward signals for generated text. We therefore compare detector sarcasm and human-authorship probabilities with LLM-judge ratings obtained without ground-truth source labels.

\paragraph{Contributions.} We construct DualTest from Chinese and English resources and use same-configuration controls to identify class-conditional negative transfer concentrated on AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic. The effect persists across both languages and when the same detectors evaluate another generator's outputs without retraining, while representation diagnostics link source-related scores and directions to the affected decisions and reveal sensitivity to a residual high-variance direction estimated from the fitting split, motivating semantic characterization and targeted causal tests. An exploratory comparison with LLM-judge ratings further motivates external validation before detector probabilities are interpreted as measures of sarcasm quality or human-likeness.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Sarcasm Detection, Synthetic Data, and Multilingual Resources}
\label{sec:related-sarcasm}

Feature-engineered methods \citep{riloff2013sarcasm}, neural sequence models \citep{ghosh2016fracking,tay2018reasoning}, and pretrained language models \citep{khatri2020,du2022pali} have each advanced sarcasm detection. Performance nevertheless varies substantially across datasets, which differ in label definitions, domains, languages, and modalities. Label schemes may reflect intended or annotator-perceived sarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval,casola2024multipico}, while multimodal resources may use modality-specific annotations \citep{yue2024sarcnet}.

Recent studies evaluate LLM-generated sarcasm through predictive performance, diversity, linguistic properties, and human judgments \citep{majer2026multi}, and associate synthetic-data characteristics with shortcut learning and poor transfer to human-authored text \citep{mahato2026llms}. These findings motivate testing whether MGTD-derived source cues interfere with sarcasm detection in a shared encoder.

\subsection{Machine-Generated Text Detection and Authorship Verification}
\label{sec:related-ai}

MGTD includes likelihood-based zero-shot methods \citep{mitchell2023detectgpt} and discriminative models trained on paired human--AI corpora \citep[HC3;][]{guo2023hc3}. M4 documents generalization gaps across generators, domains, and languages \citep{wang2024m4}, whereas RAID stress-tests unseen generators, decoding variations, and attacks \citep{dugan2024raid}. These findings are consistent with reliance on generator- or domain-specific source cues, which may also shape a shared representation used for sarcasm detection. \citet{bevendorff2025} frame realistic LLM detection as open-set authorship verification rather than closed-set attribution; here, binary human-versus-AI classification serves as a controlled auxiliary task.

To the best of our knowledge, the interaction between sarcasm detection and MGTD under shared-encoder joint training has not been directly examined. Because source is the MGTD target but should not determine sarcasm, we test whether the shared representation preserves that distinction.

\subsection{Multi-Task Negative Transfer, Shortcut Learning, and Shared-Encoder Mitigation}
\label{sec:related-transfer}

In multi-task learning, transfer depends on task relatedness and data-distribution alignment \citep{zhang2023survey,wu2020understanding}, while gradient conflict can contribute to negative transfer \citep{yu2020gradientsurgery,liu2021cagrad}. Matched single- and multi-task comparisons show similar failures and suggest that models may infer task identity from input distributions \citep{mueller2022taskconflict}; other work analyzes functional specialization in multi-task Transformers \citep{he2021stemcell,li2023functional}. A complementary shortcut-learning account is that models exploit training-predictive features that fail under distribution shift \citep{geirhos2020shortcut,ye2024spurious}. Shared representations can strengthen such correlations \citep{hu2022spurious,chai2025spurious}, while additional datasets can introduce them and reduce worst-group performance \citep{compton2023morisless}.

Mitigation approaches either isolate task-specific parameters \citep{pfeiffer2021adapterfusion,pfeiffer2020madx,mahabadi2021hyperformer,stickland2019pals} or modify information flow within a shared graph \citep{wu2019sifted,pilault2021camtl}. Our comparison of a plain shared encoder with two intermediate-representation variants follows the second direction and examines subgroup variation in negative transfer.

\subsection{Detector Outputs as Evaluation and Reward Signals}
\label{sec:related-eval}

Detector probabilities are sometimes reused as evaluation scores or generation rewards. For example, ViSP \citep{wang2026visp} includes a DIP-derived sarcasm score \citep{wen2023dip} in its PPO reward \citep{schulman2017ppo}. Such reuse assumes that a classifier's continuous output tracks the intended quality dimension, an assumption not established by classification accuracy; optimizing a model-based proxy can also diverge from an external measure \citep{gao2023overoptimization}. As LLM judges are increasingly used to evaluate generated text \citep{zheng2023judging}, we examine whether detector sarcasm probabilities agree with separately obtained LLM-judge sarcasm ratings. We also compare predicted human-authorship probabilities with judge-rated human-likeness because source classification and perceived human-likeness are related but distinct constructs.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:data}

Testing cross-task interference requires separate sarcasm and source labels, where source denotes human versus AI authorship rather than corpus. We pool the English iSarcasmEval data \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval}, CSC \citep{jang2024csc,jang2023intended}, ToSarcasm \citep{liang2022tosarcasm}, News Headlines v2 \citep{misra2023headlines,misra2021sculpting}, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus \citep{zhu2022corpus}. Since none provides both labels, we map their annotations to a common binary sarcasm space, yielding 44{,}862 instances (approximately 87\% English). From these corpora, we construct SarcTrain for sarcasm supervision, AuthTrain for source supervision, DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm evaluation, and GenTrain for generation fine-tuning (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{SarcTrain.} We reserve 2{,}103 eligible sarcastic records for GenTrain, then sample SarcTrain from the remaining 42{,}759 records. It contains 10{,}000 human-authored texts for sarcasm classification, balanced across four language $\times$ sarcasm strata with 2{,}500 texts per stratum. Three strata are downsampled; the Chinese sarcastic stratum is expanded from 2{,}396 to 2{,}500 by sampling 104 records with replacement. We assign five folds after oversampling, so some duplicates cross folds (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{AuthTrain.} AuthTrain starts from 9{,}999 approximately language-balanced SarcTrain seeds (5{,}000 English; 4{,}999 Chinese) after seed-level quality control excludes one Chinese instance. Depending on the corpus, a seed is a topic, dialogue context, non-sarcastic rewrite input, or the text itself. The locally deployed Ollama model \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} \citep{deepseek2025r1,ollama2025deepseek8b} generates AI counterparts with four language $\times$ style templates, with style selected from each seed's sarcasm label. Generation and quality control retain 9{,}996 counterparts (three unmatched seeds; 99.97\% coverage), yielding 19{,}995 source-classification texts. Source and prompt-specified style are thus distinct, approximately balanced factors for source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup analysis (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{DualTest.} DualTest contains 4{,}664 texts for source $\times$ sarcasm evaluation. Its human subset comprises the official iSarcasmEval (1{,}400 English) and ToSarcasm (972 Chinese) test sets, which are excluded from the 44{,}862-record pool. Generation and quality control retain counterparts for 2{,}292 of 2{,}372 human texts (96.6\% coverage). Human texts retain dataset labels; sarcasm labels for AI-generated texts record prompt-specified style rather than independent human ratings of perceived sarcasm. The four subgroups contain 823 human-sarcastic and 1{,}549 human-non-sarcastic texts, plus 779 AI-generated texts prompted to be sarcastic and 1{,}513 prompted to be non-sarcastic. Record-level checks find no SarcTrain or AuthTrain overlap with either side of DualTest (\Appref{app:data}, which also gives the language $\times$ source $\times$ sarcasm distribution).

\paragraph{GenTrain.} Separately, GenTrain contains 2{,}428 texts for the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) used in \Sref{sec:eval}. From 5{,}257 eligible sarcastic records, we sample 2{,}103 (40\%; 1{,}214 English and 889 Chinese) and add 325 Chinese records sampled with replacement, yielding 1{,}214 instances per language. It contains 374 rewriting, 839 response, and 1{,}215 comment instances (\Appref{app:data}).

\section{Joint and Single-Task Models}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Tasks and Architecture}
\label{sec:model-arch}

Given a Chinese or English text $x$, the model predicts binary sarcasm and source labels, with sarcastic and human-authored as the positive classes; all four combinations are valid. The tasks share a DeBERTa-v3-large encoder \citep{he2023debertav3}, allowing us to test how joint training changes the representation read by the sarcasm head. We compare three representation configurations: (1) plain sends \texttt{[CLS]} to both heads; (2) interaction-projection adds residual maps between task branches; and (3) RCNN adds recurrent and convolutional pooling. All retain the same encoder and task definitions (\Appref{app:hyper}).

First, the plain configuration serves as the shared-encoder baseline and sends \texttt{[CLS]} directly to both task heads.

Second, interaction-projection forms 256-dimensional sarcasm and MGTD vectors $h_s$ and $h_a$, updated as $h_s'=h_s+P_{a\rightarrow s}(h_a)$ and $h_a'=h_a+P_{s\rightarrow a}(h_s)$, where $P_{a\rightarrow s}$ and $P_{s\rightarrow a}$ are learned affine maps in the arrow directions; subscripts name branches and primes mark updated vectors. Each branch supplies one key--value vector, so the attention weight equals one and each $P$ composes the corresponding value and output projections.

Finally, RCNN applies a one-layer BiLSTM, parallel one-dimensional convolutions, and global max pooling, a text-classification design \citep{lai2015rcnn} used for multilingual sarcasm detection \citep{yang2024influence}. With 256 hidden units per direction, the BiLSTM yields a 512-dimensional vector per token, concatenated with the 1{,}024-dimensional encoder output. Convolutions of widths 3, 4, and 5 with 128 channels each and global max pooling produce a 384-dimensional vector $z$, followed by dropout and the two heads. \Figref{fig:architecture} illustrates this configuration.

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:model-train}

We distinguish the three representation configurations from the three training procedures: joint, sarcasm-only, and MGTD-only. Joint models have two heads; task-specific controls activate one. We compare joint and sarcasm-only procedures for plain and RCNN, holding the encoder, configuration, sarcasm data, folds, and sarcasm hyperparameters fixed. Joint training additionally uses AuthTrain batches, shared updates from both tasks, a mixed schedule, and joint early stopping. Compute exposure differs: the control traverses the full sarcasm fold each epoch, whereas joint training samples sarcasm batches within the mixed schedule. The contrast therefore covers the complete joint-training procedure.

Training alternates task-homogeneous batches at a 2:1 MGTD:sarcasm ratio, approximating the dataset sizes. Let $z_s(x)$ and $z_a(x)$ be the head inputs and $W_s$ and $W_a$ their classifiers; $s$ and $a$ denote sarcasm and authorship. Plain and RCNN share one representation, whereas interaction-projection uses $h_s'$ and $h_a'$. For active task $t$, $B_t$ contains input--label pairs $(x,y)$ and incurs only its task loss:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
\mathcal{L}(B_t)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}_{w}\bigl(W_s z_s(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subseteq \text{SarcTrain},\\[9pt]
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}\bigl(W_a z_a(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subseteq \text{AuthTrain},
\end{cases}$}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{CE}_w$ is weighted sarcasm cross-entropy with $w=[1.0,2.5]$ for the non-sarcastic and sarcastic classes, and $\mathrm{CE}$ is unweighted MGTD cross-entropy. We fix the weights before DualTest evaluation and share them across matched procedures. Both losses use logits; softmax is applied only for probability-based evaluation. Each batch updates the active head and shared parameters. The encoder uses a lower learning rate than new modules, the sarcasm-head rate is fixed across configurations, and \Appref{app:hyper} reports the MGTD-head rates. Binary predictions use a fixed 0.5 threshold unless stated otherwise.

For dual-task models, checkpoint selection accounts for both tasks. At epoch $e$, the early-stopping signal is the equally weighted sum of the two validation losses, each normalized by its first-epoch value:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stop}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}^{(e)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(1)}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(1)}},
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$ are the epoch-$e$ validation losses for the sarcasm and MGTD tasks on their respective validation sets. Normalization reduces the influence of differences in task-specific loss scales and learning dynamics. Single-task controls use their own validation loss with the same five-epoch patience (\Appref{app:hyper}).

We use five fold-matched runs. In run $k$, four SarcTrain folds train the sarcasm task and the fifth provides validation; all runs share a fixed AuthTrain 90/10 split, whose approximately 2{,}000-example holdout provides a common MGTD validation set. Each run reloads its configuration's pretrained encoder and newly initializes the remaining modules and heads; all configurations use the same folds and seed schedule. DualTest is held out until evaluation. We report mean $\pm$ standard deviation across runs to summarize variation from the sarcasm fold and initialization; for subgroup rates from separately trained final models, Wilson intervals summarize uncertainty over DualTest examples (\Tabref{tab:quadrant}; \hyperref[app:c3]{Appendix~C.3}).

For each configuration used in the subgroup analysis, we train a final model on all applicable training data for a fixed epoch count determined from the five validation runs, using an independent seed and no early stopping. These models provide the subgroup and representation analyses but are excluded from the five-run aggregates; the exploratory analysis in \Sref{sec:eval} uses the final RCNN dual-head model (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\paragraph{Loss concentration.} On AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic, FPR rises from 19.3\% under sarcasm-only training to 86.3\% under plain joint training (67.0 pp); the matched RCNN increase is 14.8 pp (19.3\% to 34.1\%). The plain-model increase on human-authored non-sarcastic texts is only 4.3 pp. The loss is therefore concentrated in the AI-generated non-sarcastic subgroup, a pattern we call class-conditional negative transfer.

\paragraph{Discrimination and score shift.} Within AI-generated texts, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) for sarcasm ranges from 0.82 to 0.88 across models. Treating prompted-sarcastic texts as positives, AUC can be interpreted as the probability that a randomly selected prompted-sarcastic text receives a higher predicted-sarcasm score than a randomly selected prompted-non-sarcastic text, with ties counted as one half. Thus, the models retain substantial threshold-free ranking ability. In the AI-generated non-sarcastic subgroup, the plain model's median predicted sarcasm probability rises from 0.204 under sarcasm-only training to 0.767 under joint training; the joint model's interquartile range, 0.635--0.839, lies above the 0.5 decision threshold. The high FPR is therefore consistent with an upward score shift rather than collapsed within-AI ranking (\hyperref[app:c3]{Appendix~C.3}).

\subsection{Cross-Language and Cross-Generator Recurrence}
\label{sec:crossgen}

The subgroup effect has the same direction in both languages. Joint--sarcasm-only FPR increases for English and Chinese are 63.5 and 79.4 pp for plain, and 16.9 and 7.7 pp for RCNN (\hyperref[app:c3]{Appendix~C.3}; \Tabref{tab:lang-quadrant}).

For generator transfer, unchanged detectors evaluate Qwen3.5-9B outputs produced with the same language $\times$ style templates (Ollama tag \texttt{qwen3.5:9b}) \citep{qwen2026qwen35}. On non-sarcastic outputs, plain joint and sarcasm-only FPRs are 91.3\% and 7.9\% (83.4 pp, versus 67.0 pp on DeepSeek), while matched RCNN rates are 22.1\% and 10.6\%. The higher joint-training FPR therefore recurs on a second generator without detector retraining (\Figref{fig:quadrant}; \hyperref[app:c3]{Appendix~C.3}; \Tabref{tab:wilson}).

\subsection{Representation-Level Diagnosis}
\label{sec:mechanism}

For each DualTest text, the frozen final plain joint model yields a single 1{,}024-dimensional pooled vector $h$ summarizing the whole text. Q1 also uses vectors from the matched sarcasm-only model; Q2 and Q3 use the joint-model $h$. An intervention changes $h$ before the unchanged sarcasm head is reapplied; the encoder and head remain frozen. The questions address source availability, association with sarcasm scores, and false-positive allocation. \Appref{app:repr} gives details.

\paragraph{Q1: Is source available?} A linear probe tests whether an attribute can be recovered from frozen representations without changing the model \citep{hewitt2019probes}. For each model, we fit logistic-regression probes to all 4{,}664 DualTest vectors and report stratified five-fold ROC AUC over the human-versus-AI labels (0.5 is chance; 1.0 is perfect ranking). AUC is 0.986 for joint-model vectors and 0.974 for sarcasm-only vectors, so source is highly linearly readable in both. The latter result shows that this decodability is not unique to MGTD supervision: systematic human--AI differences are represented even when source is not a training label.

\paragraph{Q2: Is source associated with sarcasm decisions?} A logit is a pre-softmax class score. On AI-generated non-sarcastic texts, MGTD AI and sarcasm logits correlate positively ($\rho=0.519$): higher AI logits tend to accompany higher sarcasm logits. Correlations within length terciles (0.44, 0.56, 0.63) show that coarse length differences alone do not explain this. A \emph{direction} is a vector in $h$-space; for a linear head, the class-weight difference indicates how $h$ can move to raise one class logit relative to the other. Thus, $d_{\mathrm{sarc}}=W_s[1]-W_s[0]$ raises the sarcastic-over-non-sarcastic logit, and $d_{\mathrm{AI}}=W_a[0]-W_a[1]$ raises the AI-over-human logit (MGTD class 1 is human). The source-probe direction is the normalized coefficient of a separate fitting-half logistic probe, distinct from the full-set probes in Q1.

Ordinary cosine weights dimensions equally and finds the head directions nearly orthogonal ($\cos\approx-0.03$). Covariance-aware alignment is instead a data-weighted similarity between directions, emphasizing dimensions where observed representations vary more \citep{ledoit2004covariance}. Its absolute similarity with the sarcasm direction is 0.49 for the MGTD-head direction and 0.41 for the source-probe direction. Thus, source-related scores co-vary with sarcasm scores, and the data-weighted similarities are markedly larger than ordinary cosine.

\paragraph{Q3: Do interventions change source-group false-positive allocation?} DualTest is split 50/50 within each source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup. Label-dependent directions and transforms are estimated on the fitting half, fixed, and applied to the evaluation half. Direction removal centers $h$, subtracts its projection onto a direction or subspace, restores the mean, and feeds the modified vector to the unchanged sarcasm head \citep{elazar2021amnesic}. These vectors form the transformed evaluation set; \Appref{app:repr} gives operator-specific statistics.

The FPR gap---AI-generated minus human-authored FPR on non-sarcastic texts---shows how the unchanged sarcasm head allocates errors. For each transformed set, a newly cross-validated linear probe yields residual source AUC, measuring how much human-versus-AI information remains linearly readable. The untouched within-model baseline is a 52.0-pp gap with source AUC 0.982, distinct from the earlier 67.0-pp joint--sarcasm-only difference.

\noindent\textbf{(a) Source-direction interventions.} To test whether a single learned source contrast controls the gap, we remove either the joint model's MGTD-head direction or the fitting-half source-probe direction. Their gaps are 56.0 and 51.9 pp, with source AUCs of 0.982 and 0.981. To target source information distributed across multiple linear directions, we apply INLP, which repeatedly fits a source classifier and removes its predictive direction to construct a multidimensional source-targeted subspace \citep{ravfogel2020inlp}. Removing 16 INLP directions gives a 72.1-pp gap and source AUC 0.974. These removals leave source highly readable and do not narrow the gap.

\noindent\textbf{(b) Covariance-aware intervention.} We apply LEACE, a closed-form linear transform designed to reduce linearly decodable source information while accounting for representation covariance \citep{belrose2023leace}. LEACE lowers source AUC from 0.982 to 0.589 and the gap from 52.0 to 19.1 pp, whereas ten covariance-matched random directions leave gaps of 51.4--52.7 pp. The two groups' unweighted mean FPR remains near 59\% (58.8 to 59.1) because human non-sarcastic FPR rises from 32.8 to 49.5. LEACE therefore changes how the frozen sarcasm head allocates false positives between sources.

\noindent\textbf{(c) Study-specific residual-geometry intervention.} To explore dominant geometry outside a low-dimensional source subspace, we use three INLP directions to define a study-specific residual space and extract its PC1 (\emph{INLP-3 residual PC1}). On the fitting half, we (i) fit three orthonormal INLP source directions, (ii) project centered representations onto their orthogonal complement, (iii) run PCA on the residual representations, and (iv) retain the first, highest-variance component. On the evaluation half, we remove only this PC1 from each original representation; the three INLP directions define the search space but are not removed. This intervention narrows the gap to 12.6 pp while source AUC remains 0.982. The FPR gap therefore does not consistently track residual linear source AUC, so source decodability alone is insufficient to explain the frozen head's source-group error gap.

For Q3, LEACE and INLP-3 residual PC1 narrow the source-group FPR gap, unlike the tested head, probe, and INLP removals, which neither narrow it nor substantially reduce residual source AUC. Together, Q1--Q3 show source availability, association with sarcasm scores and data-weighted directions, and sensitivity of false-positive allocation to representation geometry (\hyperref[app:repr-evidence]{Appendix~E.4}; \Tabref{tab:intervention}).

\section{Exploratory Comparison of Detector Scores with LLM-Judge Ratings}
\label{sec:eval}

We compare RCNN's human-authorship and sarcasm probabilities with human-likeness and sarcasm ratings because detector scores may be reused for quality evaluation or rewards. DeepSeek-V4-Flash \citep{deepseek2026v4flash}, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite \citep{googledeepmind2025gemini25flashlite}, and GPT-5-nano \citep{openai2025gpt5} rate paired outputs from base Qwen3-8B \citep{qwen2025} and its GenTrain-adapted QLoRA counterpart \citep{dettmers2023qlora} for 300 prompts (180 Chinese topic comments and 120 English rewrites). Ratings omit source labels and cover 600 outputs; we compare condition means and text rankings.

\paragraph{Mixed agreement by setting and level.} In Chinese generation, detector sarcasm probability rises from 0.780 to 0.895 while the three-judge mean falls from 3.99 to 3.80; both decline in English rewriting. Sarcasm ratings fall in all six setting--judge comparisons, five significantly after Benjamini--Hochberg FDR correction \citep{benjamini1995fdr}. Predicted human-authorship rises in both settings while human-likeness ratings fall. Text-level sarcasm correlations are near zero in Chinese ($-0.020$) and moderately positive in English ($+0.338$); pooled human-likeness correlation is negative ($-0.212$), driven largely by opposing condition means. Thus, only English sarcasm agrees across both levels: Chinese sarcasm has opposite condition changes and near-zero rank agreement, while human-likeness has opposite changes and negative pooled rank agreement. Detector probabilities therefore require external validation before use as quality scores or rewards. Appendix C reports tests, correlations, and means (\Tabref{tab:correlation}--\ref{tab:sft-scores}); Appendix D gives disagreement cases (\Tabref{tab:cases-auth}--\ref{tab:cases-fn}).

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We observe class-conditional negative transfer: on AI-generated non-sarcastic texts, the final plain joint model's FPR exceeds its sarcasm-only counterpart by 67.0 pp, with upward scores despite substantial within-AI discrimination. The pattern recurs across languages and on Qwen3.5 outputs; RCNN and interaction-projection have lower subgroup rates. Interventions show that error allocation depends on representation geometry beyond the MGTD direction or residual linear source AUC, motivating semantic and causal analysis. Predicted human-authorship also diverges from human-likeness ratings, requiring external validation before these probabilities serve as quality scores or rewards.

\section*{Limitations}

DualTest combines human sarcasm annotations with prompt-specified style labels for AI-generated texts. Accordingly, false positives for AI-generated texts are defined relative to the style specified in the prompt rather than independent human annotation of perceived sarcasm.

The core joint-versus-sarcasm-only comparisons evaluate the complete joint-training procedure. Within each pair, the architecture, sarcasm data, fold assignments, sarcasm-head learning rate, and class weights are held fixed, while MGTD batches, mixed-task scheduling, shared-parameter updates, and joint early stopping are introduced together. Compute- and update-matched controls could help disentangle these components. Comparisons across architectures do not isolate architecture effects because model capacity and optimization also differ. The cost-sensitive sarcasm loss may also affect absolute recall and false-positive rates.

Because oversampling precedes fold assignment, some duplicated SarcTrain records occur in different folds and may appear on both sides of a training--validation split, introducing dependence into checkpoint selection, although these records do not occur in DualTest. Generation failures from \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} leave 80 human test texts without retained AI counterparts.

The representation analyses on one separately trained final model connect source-related structure to behavior but do not isolate a unique causal feature or characterize variation across seeds. Some diagnostics use unlabeled statistics from all DualTest vectors: covariance-aware alignment uses the full-set covariance, and specified Euclidean interventions use the full-set mean. They are therefore transductive diagnostics, not fully held-out intervention estimates. The intervention split also does not preserve human--AI seed pairs, and 24.7\% of retained pairs cross the two halves, so the partitions are not fully independent at the seed level. The detector--judge comparison lacks human ratings, and language is not varied independently of generation task. Future work should use independent style annotations, matched training budgets, human ratings, and more generators.

\section*{Ethical considerations}

All human-authored texts were obtained from publicly released research datasets: iSarcasmEval, CSC, ToSarcasm, News Headlines, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus. The iSarcasmEval and CSC repositories display MIT licenses and the News Headlines v2 Kaggle release states CC BY 4.0, whereas we do not redistribute human-authored text from sources without an identified redistribution license (\Appref{app:data}). We collected no new user data or recruited participants, and we did not perform an additional audit of the released corpora for personally identifiable information or offensive content.

All AI-generated texts were produced locally using publicly released open-weight models (\texttt{deepseek-r1:8b}, Qwen3.5-9B, and the base and QLoRA-adapted Qwen3-8B). They were used only for training and evaluation and were labeled as AI-generated. For automated evaluation, evaluation inputs, generated outputs, and rating instructions were transmitted to the DeepSeek, Google, and OpenAI APIs. Dataset metadata and ground-truth source labels were not transmitted.

Given the observed subgroup error and the absence of validation for individual-text authorship attribution or high-stakes deployment, detector outputs should not be used as evidence of authorship or misconduct without human review and domain-specific validation. They should not be deployed for content moderation or automated sanctions without evaluating out-of-distribution behavior. The error concentrated on AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic is an example of systematic subgroup error affecting a particular type of generated content.

AI-assisted tools supported language editing, structural revision, and formatting, but did not generate the reported experimental outputs or statistics. The authors determined the study design, analyses, and conclusions and take full responsibility for the work.
