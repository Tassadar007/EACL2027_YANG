\begin{abstract}
Sarcasm is non-literal language whose intended meaning may diverge from apparent sentiment, complicating detection in online text. As LLM-generated content appears alongside human writing, systems may need to judge both sarcasm and machine authorship. These judgments interact in generation, where detector scores may be treated as measures of sarcastic intent and human authorship. Their interaction in shared representations remains insufficiently understood. MGTD may promote source-predictive stylistic or distributional cues, whereas sarcasm detection should reflect pragmatic intent rather than source. We conduct, to our knowledge, the first bilingual diagnosis of how joint training with MGTD changes sarcasm detection across Chinese and English. We harmonize bilingual resources, generate topic-matched AI texts, and build DualTest with source and sarcasm labels, defining AI-side sarcasm labels by requested generation style. Same-architecture joint and single-task procedures reveal class-conditional negative transfer concentrated on AI-generated text prompted to be non-sarcastic. This pattern persists with a second generator, while substantially lower rates are observed in the evaluated RCNN and interaction-projection configurations. An exploratory comparison shows that detector scores for human authorship and LLM ratings of perceived human-likeness rank generated outputs differently. These findings motivate subgroup evaluation and caution against detector-only assessment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A sarcastic sentence typically conveys an attitude or intent that differs from, and may oppose, its literal meaning. Sarcasm can invert sentiment polarity and disrupt opinion-mining systems \citep{tay2018reasoning}; ironic or sarcastic abuse is also a known challenge for content moderation \citep{vidgen2019challenges}. The task remains difficult because it depends on contrast, intent, and context. Annotation outcomes vary with elicitation method and annotator perspective \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,casola2024multipico}, while model behavior is sensitive to domain and style \citep{jang2024csc}; multilingual settings introduce further variation \citep{casola2024multipico} (\Sref{sec:related-sarcasm}).

LLM-generated text adds a source dimension to this task. Human-authored and AI-generated texts can each be sarcastic or non-sarcastic, so all four combinations are possible. A robust sarcasm detector should not infer sarcastic intent from source alone.

Machine-generated text detection (MGTD) imposes a contrasting representational demand: its objective distinguishes human-written from AI-generated text and therefore favors source-predictive representations \citep{guo2023hc3,wang2024m4} (\Sref{sec:related-ai}). Some latent stylistic or distributional cues predictive of AI-generated text may overlap with, or become entangled with, features used for sarcasm detection. The output labels do not conflict; the potential conflict lies in how the two tasks organize and use the shared representation. Sarcasm detection should not rely on source differences alone, whereas MGTD must encode them. Shared-encoder multi-task learning can suffer task conflict and negative transfer \citep{mueller2022taskconflict}; more specifically, it can exploit knowledge useful for one task but spurious for another \citep{hu2022spurious}. Evaluating the tasks only in separate models cannot reveal how incorporating MGTD into a joint-training procedure changes the sarcasm decision.

We study joint training through a controlled same-architecture comparison rather than assuming that an auxiliary task must improve accuracy. A dual-head model lets us test how sarcasm decisions change when MGTD batches additionally optimize the shared representation. Single-head controls provide a direct procedural counterpoint, and source $\times$ sarcasm subgroups show where the change appears.

As a downstream implication, we examine whether the same detectors provide reliable evaluation signals for generated sarcasm. ViSP, for example, derives PPO rewards from a sarcasm detector \citep{wang2026visp}, while generated sarcasm may be judged for both sarcastic intent and human-likeness (\Sref{sec:related-eval}). If detector scores are source-sensitive, they could favor source-predictive stylistic or distributional cues rather than measure sarcastic intent. We therefore compare detector scores for human authorship with source-blinded LLM ratings of perceived human-likeness separately in \Sref{sec:eval}.

We investigate the interaction through same-architecture comparisons. We train dual-head models on bilingual Chinese--English data and build DualTest, whose dual labels partition evaluation into four source $\times$ sarcasm quadrants. Compared with single-head controls using the same sarcasm data, the evaluated joint-training procedure substantially increases the sarcasm false-positive rate on AI-generated text prompted to be non-sarcastic. We refer to this procedure-induced degradation, concentrated in a particular source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup, as class-conditional negative transfer. The pattern persists with a second generator, with substantially lower rates in the evaluated RCNN and interaction-projection configurations than in the plain configuration.

We then examine the shared representation. Source information is highly decodable and correlates with sarcasm decisions. Representation interventions show that the subgroup gap is sensitive to a dominant-variance subspace, while leaving the causal content of that subspace unresolved (\Sref{sec:mechanism}, \Appref{app:repr}). Because probe accuracy alone does not establish how encoded information is used \citep{hewitt2019probes,elazar2021amnesic}, we treat the source-correlated shortcut as a mechanism hypothesis with preliminary support rather than an established cause.

\paragraph{Contributions.} To our knowledge, we provide the first bilingual diagnostic study of how joint training with MGTD changes sarcasm detection across Chinese and English, together with DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm analysis. Same-architecture comparisons across models and generators identify class-conditional negative transfer concentrated on AI-generated text prompted to be non-sarcastic; cross-architecture comparisons characterize how its severity varies across the evaluated configurations. Representation analyses characterize and constrain the source-shortcut hypothesis. Finally, an exploratory detector--judge comparison illustrates a downstream risk of using detector scores to evaluate generated sarcasm and perceived human-likeness.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Sarcasm Detection and Multilingual Resources}
\label{sec:related-sarcasm}

Feature-engineered methods \citep{riloff2013sarcasm}, neural sequence models \citep{ghosh2016fracking,tay2018reasoning}, and pretrained language models \citep{khatri2020,du2022pali} have each advanced sarcasm detection. The task remains difficult in multilingual and class-imbalanced settings, especially because datasets differ in whether labels reflect intended sarcasm or annotator-perceived sarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval,casola2024multipico}. Although scores are not directly comparable across datasets, languages, modalities, or evaluation setups, they illustrate substantial benchmark variability. On English iSarcasmEval \citep{abufarha2022isarcasmeval}, the top two systems report positive-class F1 of 0.605 and 0.569; across MultiPICo languages \citep{casola2024multipico}, zero-shot ChatGPT positive-class F1 ranges from 0.152 to 0.567. On the bilingual multimodal SarcNet benchmark \citep{yue2024sarcnet}, a text-only XLM-R baseline reaches 0.734 F1.

Architecturally, this work follows \citet{yang2024influence}, who compared mBERT, mBERT-BiLSTM, and mBERT-RCNN on mixed English--Arabic and English--Chinese training sets and found that the RCNN variant generally improved performance on two English test sets. Their RCNN implementation builds on the recurrent convolutional architecture of \citet{lai2015rcnn}; we therefore include RCNN among the intermediate architectures examined in \Sref{sec:model-arch}.

The benchmark results summarized above come from different datasets, so \Sref{sec:backbone} provides directly comparable mBERT dual-head baselines on DualTest. To our knowledge, existing sarcasm resources do not provide both sarcasm and human-versus-AI source labels. DualTest supplies both labels in a bilingual Chinese--English setting.

The Chinese and English source resources differ in domain, annotation procedure, and class balance, so bilinguality introduces additional distributional variation. We treat language as a robustness and stratification dimension rather than as the primary transfer objective: the study asks whether the subgroup failure is confined to one language, not whether knowledge transfers across languages. We therefore report language-stratified results in addition to pooled metrics.

\subsection{Machine-Generated Text Detection and Authorship Verification}
\label{sec:related-ai}

Machine-generated text detection (MGTD) includes zero-shot model-likelihood methods, including probability-curvature detectors \citep{mitchell2023detectgpt}, and discriminative models trained on paired human--AI corpora \citep[HC3;][]{guo2023hc3}. Generalization is a central difficulty: M4 \citep{wang2024m4} provides a benchmark across generators, domains, and languages and shows that detectors struggle on unseen domains or LLMs, while RAID \citep{dugan2024raid} stress-tests detectors under unseen generators, decoding variations, and adversarial attacks. This generalization gap is consistent with reliance on source-predictive regularities that are partly generator- or domain-specific; such cues are relevant here because they may enter a representation shared with sarcasm detection. \citet{bevendorff2025} distinguish closed-set authorship attribution from open-set authorship verification and argue that realistic LLM detection is better cast as the latter. Our binary MGTD setup is narrower: we use it as a controlled source-classification auxiliary task, not as a measure of text quality.

In our literature search, we found no work that jointly trains sarcasm detection and MGTD with a shared encoder.\footnote{Searches conducted in May 2026 and updated in July 2026 across Semantic Scholar, arXiv, ACL Anthology, and Google Scholar, using keyword combinations including ``sarcasm detection,'' ``machine-generated text detection,'' ``AI-generated text detection,'' ``multi-task learning,'' and ``joint detection.''} This gap motivates our study because source information has different roles in the two tasks: it is the target of MGTD but should not by itself determine a sarcasm detector's prediction. Our study tests whether a shared representation preserves that distinction.

\subsection{Multi-Task Negative Transfer, Shortcut Learning, and Shared-Encoder Mitigation}
\label{sec:related-transfer}

In multi-task learning, auxiliary tasks are not always beneficial: transfer depends on task relatedness and alignment between task data \citep{zhang2023survey,wu2020understanding}, while gradient conflict is a central optimization challenge that can contribute to negative transfer \citep{yu2020gradientsurgery,liu2021cagrad}. \citet{mueller2022taskconflict} provide a relevant NLP precedent through matched single- and multi-task comparisons. They find similar negative-transfer patterns in canonical and text-to-text systems and note that models may infer task identity from input distributions, a strategy that may not transfer reliably out of distribution. \citet{he2021stemcell} and \citet{li2023functional} further analyze functional specialization in multi-task Transformer encoders. Shortcut learning offers a complementary reading: models may exploit features that are predictive in the training distribution yet fail under distribution shift \citep{geirhos2020shortcut,ye2024spurious}. In MTL, shared representations can increase reliance on spurious correlations \citep{hu2022spurious,chai2025spurious}. Related multi-dataset work shows that adding data can introduce spurious correlations and reduce worst-group performance \citep{compton2023morisless}.

Mitigation under a shared encoder takes two broad forms. Parameter-efficient methods introduce task-specific or task-conditioned lightweight components (AdapterFusion, \citealp{pfeiffer2021adapterfusion}; MAD-X, \citealp{pfeiffer2020madx}; HyperFormer++, \citealp{mahabadi2021hyperformer}; PALs, \citealp{stickland2019pals}). Other methods explicitly gate or adapt information sharing within the shared computation (\citealp{wu2019sifted}; CA-MTL, \citealp{pilault2021camtl}).

Rather than isolating task-specific parameters, we examine whether alternative intermediate representations alter the subgroup failure induced by joint training. We compare a no-module baseline with two intermediate-module variants (\Sref{sec:quadrant}). Unlike aggregate negative transfer, our analysis asks whether the degradation under the evaluated joint-training procedure concentrates in particular source $\times$ sarcasm subgroups.

\subsection{Misalignment in Detector-Based Evaluation and Rewards}
\label{sec:related-eval}

The representational conflict studied above may also matter when detector outputs are reused beyond classification, particularly as evaluation or reward signals for generated text. Automated scorers, including LLM judges, are increasingly used to evaluate generated text \citep{zheng2023judging}. Model-based scores are also optimized as rewards, but overoptimization can increase a proxy score while degrading a gold-standard measure \citep{gao2023overoptimization}. A detector can achieve useful classification accuracy without its continuous output being calibrated as a measure of style strength or generation quality. Multimodal sarcasm generation may face this risk: ViSP \citep{wang2026visp} uses the DIP sarcasm detector \citep{wen2023dip} as a PPO reward \citep{schulman2017ppo}. As an exploratory downstream analysis, \hyperref[sec:eval]{Section~\ref*{sec:eval}} compares detector scores for human authorship with separately obtained, source-blinded LLM ratings of perceived human-likeness.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:data}

Testing for interference requires separate task supervision and evaluation labels for authorship and sarcasm. Here ``source'' denotes human-versus-AI authorship; ``corpus'' denotes an input dataset. We combine iSarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval}, CSC \citep{jang2024csc,jang2023intended}, ToSarcasm \citep{liang2022tosarcasm}, News Headlines v2 \citep{misra2023headlines,misra2021sculpting}, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus \citep{zhu2022corpus}. None provides both labels. Mapping their annotations to a common binary sarcasm label yields 44{,}862 pooled instances (approximately 87\% English), from which we construct three training sets and DualTest (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Sarcasm classification training set, SarcTrain (10{,}000, all human).} Each language $\times$ label bucket contains 2{,}500 instances. Three buckets are downsampled; the Chinese sarcastic bucket starts from 2{,}396 records representing 2{,}394 unique texts and adds 104 oversampled records. The resulting set is organized into five folds.

\paragraph{MGTD training set, AuthTrain (19{,}995).} From 9{,}999 language-balanced human seeds, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (Ollama tag \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b}) \citep{deepseek2025r1,ollama2025deepseek8b} produces 9{,}996 retained outputs (99.97\% pairing coverage); \Appref{app:data} details quality-control exclusions.

Four language $\times$ style templates follow the balanced seed labels. Authorship and requested-style labels are constructed as separate, approximately balanced factors; no independence is assumed for realized textual features or perceived sarcasm (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Generation training set, GenTrain (2{,}428; used in \Sref{sec:eval}).} From 5{,}257 eligible positive-sarcasm records in iSarcasm and ToSarcasm, seed-42 sampling selects 2{,}103 records (1{,}214 English; 889 Chinese). Chinese oversampling adds 325 records, yielding 1{,}214 per language. The resulting set contains 374 rewriting, 839 response, and 1{,}215 comment instances and serves as the instruction-tuning data for SFT (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Diagnostic test set, DualTest (4{,}664).} The human subset combines official iSarcasm (1{,}400 English) and ToSarcasm (972 Chinese) test sets. Generation quality control retains AI counterparts for 2{,}292 of 2{,}372 texts (96.6\% coverage). Human texts retain their annotations; for AI texts, the sarcasm label records the prompted condition rather than an independently annotated judgment of perceived sarcasm.

The four source $\times$ sarcasm groups contain 823 human-sarcastic, 1{,}549 human-non-sarcastic, 779 AI-sarcastic, and 1{,}513 AI-non-sarcastic instances. \Appref{app:data} gives the language $\times$ source $\times$ sarcasm distribution.

\section{Dual-Head Detection Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Tasks and Architecture}
\label{sec:model-arch}

Given a Chinese or English input text $x$, the model produces two separate, non-mutually-exclusive binary predictions: whether $x$ is labeled sarcastic and whether its authorship source is labeled human or AI. Any combination of the two labels is valid. The tasks share a single DeBERTa-v3-large encoder \citep{he2023debertav3}, allowing us to test how incorporating MGTD into joint training changes the representation read by the sarcasm head. This encoder was pretrained on English corpora; \Sref{sec:backbone} directly compares it with multilingual-pretrained mBERT / XLM-R on the same bilingual test set. Encoder outputs are aggregated by an intermediate module and then passed to two task-specific classification heads; \Figref{fig:architecture} shows the RCNN configuration.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_architecture.pdf}
  \caption{RCNN dual-head configuration: a shared DeBERTa-v3-large encoder, an RCNN intermediate module (dashed), and two task-specific heads. The comparisons in \Sref{sec:exp} remove this component in the plain configuration or replace it with task-branch interaction projection, while retaining the same encoder backbone and task definitions (\Appref{app:hyper}).}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The RCNN configuration uses BiLSTM $+$ CNN as its intermediate module. A single-layer bidirectional LSTM with hidden size 256 per direction produces a 512-dimensional output at each token position. We concatenate it with the 1{,}024-dimensional encoder output, then apply three groups of one-dimensional convolutions with kernel widths 3/4/5 and 128 channels each. Global max pooling produces a 384-dimensional vector $z$. After dropout, $z$ is passed to the two heads. This module adapts the mBERT-RCNN intermediate architecture of \citet{yang2024influence} to a DeBERTa-based dual-head setting.

To test the intermediate layer's role, \Sref{sec:exp} compares two variants. The plain model sends \texttt{[CLS]} directly to both heads. The interaction-projection model forms two learned 256-dimensional projections, $h_s$ and $h_a$, and updates them as $h_s'=h_s+P_{a\rightarrow s}(h_a)$ and $h_a'=h_a+P_{s\rightarrow a}(h_s)$. Each branch contains one vector, so no query--key interaction remains; $P$ is the effective learned value/output projection. The configurations retain the same encoder and task definitions while testing distinct intermediate-representation designs (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:model-train}

Training uses a mixed loader that supplies task-homogeneous batches according to a 2:1 MGTD:sarcasm schedule, matching the approximate data-size ratio between AuthTrain and SarcTrain. Let $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ denote parameters updated by both tasks, let $z_s(x)$ and $z_a(x)$ be the representations read by the sarcasm and MGTD heads, and let $W_s$ and $W_a$ denote the two classification heads. The two representations coincide in the plain and RCNN configurations; in interaction projection they are the task-specific $h_s'$ and $h_a'$ defined above. At step $t$, the loss is computed only for the task corresponding to the current batch $B_t$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
\mathcal{L}(B_t)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}_{w}\bigl(W_s z_s(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{SarcTrain},\\[9pt]
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}\bigl(W_a z_a(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{AuthTrain},
\end{cases}$}
\end{equation}

where $\mathrm{CE}_w$ is a pre-specified cost-sensitive cross-entropy for sarcasm (weights are provided in \Appref{app:hyper}); because SarcTrain is class-balanced, the weighting emphasizes errors on sarcastic examples rather than correcting training-set imbalance. Softmax is applied to logits only when probabilities are required. The gradient flow follows from the equation: on sarcasm batches, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_a = 0$ (symmetrically, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_s = 0$ on MGTD batches), whereas $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ receives gradients from both types of batches. Unless otherwise stated, binary predictions use a fixed probability threshold of 0.5 rather than a threshold tuned on DualTest.

The encoder uses a lower learning rate than newly added modules. The sarcasm-head rate is fixed across configurations; MGTD-head rates are reported in \Appref{app:hyper}.

Model selection accounts for both tasks. At epoch $e$, the early-stopping signal is the equally weighted sum of the two validation losses, each normalized by its first-epoch value:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stop}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}^{(e)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(1)}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(1)}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$ are the epoch-$e$ validation losses for the sarcasm and MGTD tasks on their respective validation sets. Normalization reduces the influence of differences in task-specific loss scales and learning dynamics.

Five runs are defined by the SarcTrain validation folds: four folds train and one validates sarcasm, while AuthTrain uses a fixed 90/10 split. Each run reloads the pretrained encoder and randomly initializes new modules and heads. Configurations share fold assignments and nominal seeds, enabling split-matched comparisons but not identical initialization.

DualTest enters no training or validation split. Five-run standard deviations combine fold and initialization variation. Wilson intervals are conditional on a fixed final model and cover test-instance, not training or generator, uncertainty (\Tabref{tab:quadrant}; Appendix~C.7).

Each subgroup configuration has a final model trained on all applicable data with a fixed epoch count determined from the five validation runs, an independent seed, and no early stopping. Their weights and training duration are not selected using DualTest, and they are not used in five-run aggregates; \Sref{sec:eval} uses the final RCNN dual-head model (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\section{Experiments and Diagnosis}
\label{sec:exp}

The experiments move from aggregate comparison to dual--single ablation and subgroup diagnosis. We first compare pretrained encoders and intermediate configurations, then measure the change between the evaluated joint- and single-task procedures, and finally partition predictions into source $\times$ sarcasm quadrants. DualTest is used for these descriptive and diagnostic comparisons, but not for training, validation, threshold selection, or epoch selection. Aggregate results are five-run means $\pm$ standard deviations; fixed final models support paired instance-level subgroup comparisons and Wilson intervals.

\subsection{Backbone Comparison}
\label{sec:backbone}

We compare dual-head mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa-base, DeBERTa-v3-base, and DeBERTa-v3-large under one evaluation protocol, adding the two intermediate variants on the large encoder. Their pretraining spans multilingual Wikipedia \citep{devlin2019bert}, multilingual CommonCrawl \citep{conneau2020xlmr}, and primarily English DeBERTa-v3 pretraining with an ELECTRA-style objective \citep{he2023debertav3,clark2020electra}. Positive-class F1 uses the sarcastic class for sarcasm and the human class for MGTD on full DualTest.

\Tabref{tab:backbone} shows higher mean F1 for the evaluated DeBERTa configurations than for mBERT and XLM-R, with RCNN obtaining the highest means. The intermediate variants also show 34--47\% lower observed standard deviations across the five runs. Because backbone scale, pretraining, and intermediate representation vary together, this comparison characterizes the evaluated configurations rather than isolating one factor. RCNN reaches 0.6309 sarcasm F1 and 0.9301 MGTD F1, establishing a strong shared-encoder diagnostic configuration while leaving a substantial task-level performance gap to explain. Its sarcasm F1 is 0.819 in Chinese and 0.429 in English; prevalence, genre, annotation, and language jointly shape this contrast. \Appref{app:results} provides macro-averaged, human-subset, and language-stratified results.

\subsection{Four-Quadrant Diagnosis: Class-Conditional Negative Transfer across Intermediate Architectures}
\label{sec:quadrant}

Aggregate F1 hides whether errors depend jointly on source and sarcasm class. We therefore partition DualTest by source $\times$ sarcasm. Human labels are dataset annotations and AI labels record prompted conditions; under these operational task labels, single-class quadrants use recall for sarcastic subsets and false-positive rate for non-sarcastic subsets.

\subsubsection{Three Architectures and Single-Head Controls}
\label{sec:quadrant-results}

The three dual-head variants show how the subgroup pattern changes with intermediate representation. Plain and RCNN single-head models provide same-architecture controls for the joint-training comparison. The plain dual-head model predicts sarcasm for 86.3\% of AI $\times$ non-sarcastic instances. The observed rates are 40.9\% for interaction projection and 34.1\% for RCNN, while both same-architecture single-head controls yield 19.3\%. Thus, the observed severity is more than 45 pp lower in the two evaluated intermediate configurations than in the plain dual-head configuration.

On the same texts, plain dual versus single gives 1{,}021/7 discordant pairs ($p<.001$). The same-architecture single-head model receives the same all-human sarcasm supervision but not the joint MGTD procedure, tying the large increase to the evaluated joint-training procedure. RCNN's same-architecture comparison shows the same direction with a smaller gap; interaction projection has no single-head counterpart and contributes complementary configuration-level evidence.

No analogous extreme subgroup error appears in the MGTD head: its false-positive rate is 6.1\% for plain and 6.6\% for RCNN. Within AI texts, sarcasm AUC remains 0.82--0.88 while joint training raises score levels and largely preserves ordering. The failure is therefore not a collapse of within-AI ranking, but a source-conditioned upward shift that produces class-conditional negative transfer on the non-sarcastic prompted subset (\hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}).

The matched plain comparison makes the concentration especially clear: its non-sarcastic false-positive rate changes by 4.2 pp on human text but by 67.0 pp on AI-generated text. The aggregate F1 decrease is therefore driven by a sharply localized source $\times$ class effect rather than a uniform loss across DualTest.

\subsubsection{Mechanism Hypothesis and Representation-Level Diagnostics}
\label{sec:mechanism}

We hypothesize that MGTD-shaped representations expose source-correlated cues to a sarcasm head that receives no sarcasm supervision on AI text. Source is highly decodable in both dual-head (AUC 0.986) and same-architecture single-head (0.974) representations, so its presence alone is not the explanation. In the dual-head model, however, the MGTD-head AI logit correlates with the sarcasm-head logit on the target subgroup ($\rho=0.519$), while both MGTD-head and probe directions align with the sarcasm direction in whitened geometry.

Representation interventions further characterize the effective variation. LEACE \citep{belrose2023leace} reduces measured linear source decodability from AUC 0.982 to 0.589 and shifts the non-sarcastic false-positive rates from 84.8\%/32.8\% on AI/human text to 68.7\%/49.5\%. Removing a high-variance direction orthogonal to the estimated linear source direction yields 66.7\%/54.1\% while preserving measured source decodability; ten sampled random controls show no comparable source-specific shift. Under LEACE, the unweighted mean across the two source groups remains nearly unchanged (58.8\% to 59.1\%), showing error redistribution rather than overall mitigation. Together, these results support a bounded source-correlated shortcut hypothesis and show prediction sensitivity to high-variance directions without isolating a unique causal feature (\Appref{app:repr}).

This evidence chain goes beyond probe accuracy alone: task-head logit correlation connects source-related behavior to the decision, targeted interventions change how errors are allocated across sources, and the orthogonal and random controls constrain the interpretation. The result is a representation-level diagnosis of score sensitivity rather than a causal localization inferred from output labels alone.

\subsubsection{Cross-Generator Replication}
\label{sec:crossgen}

We regenerate only DualTest's AI side with Qwen3.5-9B (Ollama tag \texttt{qwen3.5:9b}) \citep{qwen2026qwen35}, retaining the human texts and trained detectors. Among 1{,}550 non-sarcastic-condition outputs, plain dual rises from 86.3\% to 91.3\% and its same-architecture single falls from 19.3\% to 7.9\%, increasing the gap from 67.0 to 83.4 pp. RCNN and interaction projection remain lower at 22.1\% and 29.5\%. Thus, both generators preserve the large plain dual--single separation and the lower rates of the two intermediate configurations. The second generator reproduces the gap direction with a different magnitude (\hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}).

\section{Exploratory Comparison of Detector Scores and LLM-Judge Ratings}
\label{sec:eval}

As a downstream implication, we test whether source-classification probability tracks perceived human-likeness and whether sarcasm probability tracks LLM ratings. Human-authorship probability and perceived human-likeness are distinct constructs; their comparison directly tests whether a classifier score can function as a generation-evaluation proxy. We compare 600 outputs from Qwen3-8B \citep{qwen2025} before and after QLoRA fine-tuning on GenTrain. The RCNN detector and three LLM judges \citep{deepseek2024v3,gemini2025report,openai2026gpt5} score outputs from 180 Chinese topic-comment and 120 English rewriting prompts; the judges are not shown the ground-truth source, and language is coupled with generation task.

Agreement varies by dimension and language. In Chinese, SFT raises detector sarcasm from 0.780 to 0.895 but lowers mean judge rating from 4.01 to 3.71; sarcasm correlation is $-0.020$ in Chinese and $+0.338$ in English. SFT outputs also receive higher human-authorship probabilities but lower perceived human-likeness ratings, yielding a pooled correlation of $-0.212$. Within-condition correlations show that this negative association is driven mainly by the reversal between base and SFT conditions.

This condition-level reversal is directly relevant to generation evaluation: fine-tuning moves the detector scores in the apparently favorable direction while moving the external LLM ratings downward. A detector can therefore provide a useful classification boundary without its continuous score preserving the ordering required of a style or quality measure.

Inter-judge agreement is low to moderate at the instance level, but five of six language $\times$ judge comparisons assign lower sarcasm ratings to SFT than to the base outputs. A plain-detector control changes the reported correlations by at most 0.02. The consistent condition-level directions therefore show that detector probabilities require external validation as generation-quality proxies or rewards. Full protocols, tests, and representative cases are reported in \hyperref[app:results]{Appendices~\ref*{app:results}}--\hyperref[app:cases]{\ref*{app:cases}}.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Source and sarcasm labels can coexist, yet joint training can change the sarcasm decision. Relative to a same-architecture single-head control using the same sarcasm data, the plain dual-head model has a sharply higher false-positive rate on AI text generated under non-sarcastic instructions. A second generator reproduces the gap's direction but not its magnitude, and both intermediate configurations have lower rates than the plain dual-head model. Representation analyses link the shift to source-correlated, high-variance directions, while revealing error redistribution rather than overall improvement and leaving causality unresolved. Detector probabilities and LLM-judge ratings also differ by dimension and task--language setting, so the evaluated scores require external validation as measures of style realization or quality. In our setting, aggregate metrics should be supplemented with authorship-source $\times$ reference-class analysis.

\section*{Limitations}

DualTest combines original sarcasm annotations for human texts with instruction-conditioned style labels for AI texts. Because instruction compliance is not independently annotated, an apparent AI-side false positive can reflect detector error or realized sarcasm despite a non-sarcastic instruction. Generation failures also leave 80 human texts without retained main-generator counterparts, so source-side comparisons are not fully paired. Seven exact train--test records on the AI side correspond to three unique DualTest texts; removing those texts changes each core AI $\times$ non-sarcastic rate by less than 0.1 pp, while retraining would be required to remove their training-side influence. Some duplicated SarcTrain texts also span training and validation folds, which can affect checkpoint selection but does not introduce those records into DualTest.

The MGTD training text is produced with one generator, and the second generator replaces only DualTest's AI side. The results therefore establish a directional replication across two retained output sets rather than coverage of broader model families, scales, or training paradigms. The input corpora also operationalize sarcasm differently, and the detector does not use conversational context.

DualTest supports both the descriptive architecture comparison and the subsequent subgroup diagnosis, so cross-configuration results are descriptive rather than an independent model-selection test. The sarcasm loss also upweights the positive class despite balanced SarcTrain; this cost-sensitive choice can affect absolute false-positive rates, although each matched dual--single comparison uses the same weight. The dual and single procedures share architecture, sarcasm data, folds, and task-specific learning rates, but differ in task schedule, total shared-encoder updates, and early stopping; they estimate the complete joint-training procedure rather than a compute-matched auxiliary-gradient effect. Cross-architecture comparisons are neither capacity- nor optimization-matched, their MGTD-head learning rates differ, and interaction projection lacks a single-head counterpart.

The mechanism evidence is primarily behavioral and linear and uses one final plain dual-head model. Source is also highly decodable from the single-head representation, so decodability alone does not explain the joint-training-specific shift. The orthogonal high-variance control prevents attribution of the intervention effect specifically to source identity, and the interventions mainly redistribute non-sarcastic errors from AI to human text rather than lowering their source-averaged rate.

The generation analysis compares human-authorship probability with perceived human-likeness, which are related but non-equivalent constructs. It uses three LLM judges rather than human evaluators, and their instance-level agreement is low to moderate. The Chinese and English subsets differ in sample size, generation task, prompt structure, and source data. The results therefore show condition-level directional differences between the evaluated detector scores and selected LLM-judge ratings, not human-perceived quality or a pure language effect.

Priority extensions are independent annotation of AI-side style realization, capacity- and training-budget-matched controls, and multi-generator, multilingual, and calibration analyses. Reward-based generation and nonlinear causal tests provide subsequent directions.

\section*{Ethical considerations}

All human-authored texts come from publicly released research datasets: iSarcasm, CSC, ToSarcasm, News Headlines, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus. Explicit repository licenses were available for iSarcasm and CSC; for sources without an identified redistribution license, we do not redistribute the human-authored text (\Appref{app:data}). We collect no new user data. We did not conduct an additional personally identifiable information or offensive-content audit beyond the preparation reflected in the released corpora.

All AI texts were generated locally using publicly released open-weight models (DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B and Qwen3.5-9B). They are used only for training and evaluation and are labeled as AI-generated. The study recruits no new human participants, but analyzes human-authored texts from previously released datasets. The automated evaluation transmits each evaluation input, generated output, and rating instructions to the DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, and GPT-5-nano APIs; it does not transmit dataset metadata or ground-truth source labels.

The detectors are developed for research diagnosis. Their outputs should not be used as evidence of authorship or misconduct at the individual-text level without human review and domain-specific validation, nor deployed for content moderation or automated sanctions without evaluating out-of-distribution behavior. The class-conditional failure diagnosed here is an example of systematic subgroup error affecting a particular type of generated content.

AI-assisted tools were used for grammar correction, wording suggestions, and formatting. They did not generate the reported experimental outputs or statistics; the authors determined the study design, analysis, and conclusions and take full responsibility for the work.
