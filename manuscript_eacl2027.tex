\begin{abstract}
Sarcasm detection requires inferring intent beyond literal wording. LLM-generated examples may expand sarcasm resources, but evaluating models on such data requires separating intended style from source-conditioned behavior. When sarcasm and source predictions are desired, a shared dual-head encoder can reuse one representation; this sharing may allow source cues learned for machine-generated text detection (MGTD) to influence sarcasm decisions. We harmonize Chinese and English resources, generate topic-matched AI text, and build DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm analysis, using requested styles as AI-side sarcasm labels. In the plain configuration, the false-positive rate relative to requested-style labels on AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic is 67.0 percentage points higher under the evaluated joint-training procedure than under a same-architecture single-task control. Within AI-generated texts, relative ranking remains largely preserved, indicating a source-conditioned score shift. The matched plain dual--single contrast retains the same direction under language stratification and fixed-detector evaluation of a second generator's outputs. Representation diagnostics support a source-correlated shortcut hypothesis. An exploratory analysis shows that predicted human-source probabilities do not consistently track source-label-blinded LLM ratings of human-likeness. These findings motivate source $\times$ class subgroup evaluation and external validation before detector probabilities are reused as quality scores or rewards.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Sarcasm is a pragmatic phenomenon in which a speaker's intended attitude may differ from an utterance's literal meaning. Interpreting it relies on implicit contrast, communicative intent, and context, making it difficult to detect automatically. Detection errors can reverse sentiment predictions and disrupt opinion-mining or content-moderation systems \citep{tay2018reasoning,vidgen2019challenges}. Annotation outcomes also vary with elicitation method and annotator perspective \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,casola2024multipico}, while model behavior is sensitive to domain, style, and language \citep{jang2024csc,casola2024multipico} (\Sref{sec:related-sarcasm}).

One potential way to expand sarcasm resources is to generate additional examples with LLMs. Their usefulness depends on whether they express the intended style without allowing source-specific regularities to become shortcuts. Evaluation must therefore separate sarcasm behavior by source: human-authored and AI-generated texts can each be sarcastic or non-sarcastic, yielding all four combinations.

Source and sarcasm can be predicted by separate models. When both outputs are desired, however, a shared encoder with task-specific heads can reuse one representation rather than duplicate encoders. This convenience may couple the tasks. Machine-generated text detection (MGTD) must distinguish human-written from AI-generated text and thus favors source-predictive representations \citep{guo2023hc3,wang2024m4} (\Sref{sec:related-ai}); these stylistic or distributional cues may overlap with features used for sarcasm detection. The labels do not conflict; the potential conflict lies in how the tasks organize a shared representation. MGTD must encode source, whereas sarcasm detection should reflect intent rather than source. Shared-encoder multi-task learning can suffer task conflict and negative transfer by exploiting information useful for one task but spurious for another \citep{mueller2022taskconflict,hu2022spurious}. We therefore test whether joint training changes sarcasm predictions.

Source-predictive regularities and sarcasm cues may vary across language and corpus settings \citep{jang2024csc,casola2024multipico,wang2024m4}. We therefore conduct the diagnosis in Chinese and English and report language-stratified results to assess the robustness of the subgroup pattern across the two settings.

To test how joint MGTD training changes sarcasm behavior, we compare same-architecture dual- and single-head models and build DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup analysis. The evaluated joint-training procedure sharply increases the requested-style false-positive rate on AI text prompted to be non-sarcastic; we call this class-conditional negative transfer. Within AI-generated texts, relative ranking remains largely preserved; joint training instead shifts sarcasm scores upward.

We then examine the shared representation. Source information is highly decodable and correlates with sarcasm decisions. To connect encoded information to actual decisions beyond probe accuracy \citep{hewitt2019probes,elazar2021amnesic}, we combine probes, task-head correlations, and representation interventions. These analyses identify a non-random high-variance direction to which the subgroup gap is sensitive, supporting a source-correlated shortcut hypothesis and providing a focused target for further causal analysis (\Sref{sec:mechanism}, \Appref{app:repr}).

Under fixed-detector evaluation of Qwen3.5-generated replacements, the matched plain dual--single contrast retains the same direction (\Sref{sec:crossgen}).

The dual-head design also has a downstream implication. Detector scores can become generation rewards: ViSP derives PPO rewards from a sarcasm detector \citep{wang2026visp}. Yet useful generated sarcasm must satisfy two distinct objectives: conveying sarcastic intent and being perceived as human-like. A dual-head detector may appear to provide corresponding signals through sarcasm and predicted human-source probabilities, but predicted human-source probability is not itself a measure of human-likeness. We examine this implication exploratorily by comparing detector scores with source-label-blinded LLM ratings of sarcasm and perceived human-likeness (\Sref{sec:eval}).

\paragraph{Contributions.} We construct DualTest from Chinese and English resources for source $\times$ sarcasm diagnosis of joint MGTD--sarcasm training. Same-architecture dual--single comparisons reveal class-conditional negative transfer under the evaluated joint-training procedure, concentrated on AI texts prompted to be non-sarcastic and characterized by a source-conditioned score shift; the direction persists under fixed-detector evaluation of a second generator. Representation diagnostics support a source-correlated shortcut hypothesis and identify a focused target for further causal analysis. An exploratory detector--judge comparison illustrates the risk of treating detector probabilities as measures of sarcasm quality or human-likeness.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Sarcasm Detection, Synthetic Data, and Multilingual Resources}
\label{sec:related-sarcasm}

Feature-engineered methods \citep{riloff2013sarcasm}, neural sequence models \citep{ghosh2016fracking,tay2018reasoning}, and pretrained language models \citep{khatri2020,du2022pali} have each advanced sarcasm detection. Performance nevertheless varies substantially across datasets because label definitions, domains, languages, and modalities differ, particularly in whether labels reflect intended or annotator-perceived sarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval,casola2024multipico,yue2024sarcnet}.

Recent work evaluates LLM-generated sarcasm directly: \citet{majer2026multi} use predictive, linguistic, and human-judgment criteria, while \citet{mahato2026llms} link synthetic-data signatures to shortcut learning and poor transfer to human-authored texts. Building on these findings, we study shared-encoder joint MGTD--sarcasm training against same-architecture sarcasm-only controls.

RCNNs combine recurrent context with convolutional pooling for text classification \citep{lai2015rcnn} and have also been evaluated for multilingual sarcasm detection \citep{yang2024influence}; we therefore include an RCNN intermediate configuration in \Sref{sec:model-arch}.

DualTest operationalizes this question with topic-matched human/AI data spanning all four source $\times$ sarcasm groups in Chinese and English; \Sref{sec:backbone} reports directly comparable mBERT dual-head baselines under a shared evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Machine-Generated Text Detection and Authorship Verification}
\label{sec:related-ai}

MGTD comprises zero-shot model-likelihood methods, such as probability-curvature detectors \citep{mitchell2023detectgpt}, and discriminative models trained on paired human--AI corpora \citep[HC3;][]{guo2023hc3}. Generalization is a central difficulty: M4 \citep{wang2024m4} provides a benchmark across generators, domains, and languages and shows that detectors struggle on unseen domains or LLMs, while RAID \citep{dugan2024raid} stress-tests detectors under unseen generators, decoding variations, and adversarial attacks. This generalization gap is consistent with reliance on source-predictive regularities that are partly generator- or domain-specific; such cues are relevant here because they may enter a representation shared with sarcasm detection. \citet{bevendorff2025} distinguish closed-set authorship attribution from open-set authorship verification and argue that realistic LLM detection is better cast as the latter. Our binary MGTD setup is narrower: we use it as a controlled source-classification auxiliary task, not as a measure of text quality.

We are not aware of prior work that directly studies shared-encoder joint training for sarcasm and MGTD.\footnote{Searches conducted in May 2026 and updated in July 2026 across Semantic Scholar, arXiv, ACL Anthology, and Google Scholar, using keyword combinations including ``sarcasm detection,'' ``synthetic sarcasm,'' ``LLM-generated sarcasm,'' ``machine-generated text detection,'' ``AI-generated text detection,'' ``multi-task learning,'' and ``joint detection.''} This gap matters because source information has different roles in the two tasks: it is the target of MGTD but should not by itself determine a sarcasm detector's prediction. Our study tests whether a shared representation preserves that distinction.

\subsection{Multi-Task Negative Transfer, Shortcut Learning, and Shared-Encoder Mitigation}
\label{sec:related-transfer}

In multi-task learning, auxiliary tasks are not always beneficial: transfer depends on task relatedness and alignment between their data distributions \citep{zhang2023survey,wu2020understanding}, while gradient conflict is a central optimization challenge that can contribute to negative transfer \citep{yu2020gradientsurgery,liu2021cagrad}. \citet{mueller2022taskconflict} provide a relevant NLP precedent through matched single- and multi-task comparisons. They find similar negative-transfer patterns in canonical and text-to-text systems and note that models may infer task identity from input distributions, a strategy that may not transfer reliably out of distribution. \citet{he2021stemcell} and \citet{li2023functional} further analyze functional specialization in multi-task Transformer encoders. Shortcut learning offers a complementary reading: models may exploit features that are predictive in the training distribution yet fail under distribution shift \citep{geirhos2020shortcut,ye2024spurious}. In MTL, shared representations can increase reliance on spurious correlations \citep{hu2022spurious,chai2025spurious}. Related multi-dataset work shows that adding data can introduce spurious correlations and reduce worst-group performance \citep{compton2023morisless}.

Shared-encoder mitigation either isolates task-specific parameters \citep{pfeiffer2021adapterfusion,pfeiffer2020madx,mahabadi2021hyperformer,stickland2019pals} or modifies information flow within a shared computation graph \citep{wu2019sifted,pilault2021camtl}. Our analysis is closer to the second design direction: we compare a no-module baseline with two intermediate-representation variants and test whether degradation changes across source $\times$ sarcasm subgroups (\Sref{sec:quadrant}).

\subsection{Misalignment in Detector-Based Evaluation and Rewards}
\label{sec:related-eval}

The representational conflict studied above may also matter when detector outputs are reused beyond classification, particularly as evaluation or reward signals for generated text. Automated scorers, including LLM judges, are increasingly used to evaluate generated text \citep{zheng2023judging}. Model-based scores are also optimized as rewards, but overoptimization can increase a proxy score while degrading a gold-standard measure \citep{gao2023overoptimization}. A detector can achieve useful classification accuracy without its continuous output being calibrated as a measure of style strength or generation quality. Multimodal sarcasm generation may face this risk: ViSP \citep{wang2026visp} uses the DIP sarcasm detector \citep{wen2023dip} as a PPO reward \citep{schulman2017ppo}. As an exploratory downstream analysis, \hyperref[sec:eval]{Section~\ref*{sec:eval}} compares predicted human-source probabilities with separately obtained, source-label-blinded LLM ratings of perceived human-likeness.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:data}

Testing for interference requires separate task supervision and evaluation labels for source and sarcasm. Here ``source'' denotes whether a text is human-authored or AI-generated; ``corpus'' denotes an input dataset. We combine iSarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval}, CSC \citep{jang2024csc,jang2023intended}, ToSarcasm \citep{liang2022tosarcasm}, News Headlines v2 \citep{misra2023headlines,misra2021sculpting}, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus \citep{zhu2022corpus}. None of these datasets provides both labels. Mapping their annotations to a common binary sarcasm label yields 44{,}862 pooled instances (approximately 87\% English). From this pool, we construct separate resources for sarcasm supervision (SarcTrain), source supervision (AuthTrain), generation fine-tuning (GenTrain), and source $\times$ sarcasm evaluation (DualTest; \Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{SarcTrain.} SarcTrain contains 10{,}000 human-authored texts for sarcasm classification. Each language $\times$ label bucket contains 2{,}500 instances. Three buckets are downsampled; the Chinese sarcastic bucket starts from 2{,}396 records and adds 104 oversampled records. The resulting set is organized into five folds.

\paragraph{AuthTrain.} AuthTrain contains 19{,}995 texts for source classification. After seed-level generation quality control excludes one Chinese SarcTrain instance from use as a seed, AuthTrain retains 9{,}999 approximately language-balanced human seeds (5{,}000 English / 4{,}999 Chinese). The Ollama model tagged \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} \citep{deepseek2025r1,ollama2025deepseek8b} produces 9{,}996 retained AI counterparts; three selected human seeds remain unmatched, giving 99.97\% pairing coverage. \Appref{app:data} details the exclusions.

We use four language $\times$ style templates, with each seed's label determining the requested style. Source and requested-style labels form two separately controlled, approximately balanced factors, enabling source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup analysis (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{GenTrain.} GenTrain contains 2{,}428 texts for the supervised fine-tuning (SFT) used in \Sref{sec:eval}. From 5{,}257 eligible positive-sarcasm records in iSarcasm and ToSarcasm, we sample 2{,}103 records (40\%; 1{,}214 English and 889 Chinese). Chinese oversampling adds 325 records, yielding 1{,}214 per language. The resulting set contains 374 rewriting, 839 response, and 1{,}215 comment instances (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{DualTest.} DualTest contains 4{,}664 texts for source $\times$ sarcasm evaluation. Its human subset combines official iSarcasm (1{,}400 English) and ToSarcasm (972 Chinese) test sets. After generation quality control, AI counterparts remain for 2{,}292 of 2{,}372 texts (96.6\% coverage). Human texts retain their annotations; for AI texts, the sarcasm label records the prompted condition rather than an independently annotated judgment of perceived sarcasm.

The four source $\times$ sarcasm groups contain 823 human-sarcastic, 1{,}549 human-non-sarcastic, 779 AI-sarcastic, and 1{,}513 AI-non-sarcastic instances. \Appref{app:data} gives the language $\times$ source $\times$ sarcasm distribution.

\section{Joint and Single-Task Models}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Tasks and Architecture}
\label{sec:model-arch}

Given a Chinese or English input text $x$, the model produces two separate, non-mutually-exclusive binary predictions: whether $x$ is labeled sarcastic and whether its source label is human or AI. Any combination of the two labels is valid. The tasks share a single DeBERTa-v3-large encoder \citep{he2023debertav3}, allowing us to test how incorporating MGTD into joint training changes the representation read by the sarcasm head. We compare three shared-representation configurations and examine how subgroup errors vary across them; \Figref{fig:architecture} shows the RCNN configuration.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_architecture.pdf}
  \caption{RCNN dual-head configuration: a shared DeBERTa-v3-large encoder, an RCNN intermediate module (dashed), and two task-specific heads. The comparisons in \Sref{sec:exp} remove this component in the plain configuration or replace it with a task-branch interaction-projection module, while retaining the same encoder backbone and task definitions (\Appref{app:hyper}).}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The RCNN configuration uses BiLSTM $+$ CNN as its intermediate module. A single-layer bidirectional LSTM with hidden size 256 per direction produces a 512-dimensional output at each token position. We concatenate this output with the 1{,}024-dimensional encoder output, then apply three groups of one-dimensional convolutions with kernel widths 3/4/5 and 128 channels each. Global max pooling produces a 384-dimensional vector $z$. After dropout, $z$ is passed to the two heads, yielding a DeBERTa-based dual-head RCNN configuration.

Alongside RCNN, \Sref{sec:exp} compares two further configurations. The plain model sends \texttt{[CLS]} directly to both heads. The interaction-projection model forms two learned 256-dimensional projections, $h_s$ and $h_a$, and updates them as $h_s'=h_s+P_{a\rightarrow s}(h_a)$ and $h_a'=h_a+P_{s\rightarrow a}(h_s)$. Because each branch supplies only one key--value vector, every attention head assigns a weight of one to that vector; the module therefore reduces to residual cross-branch affine projections, where $P_{a\rightarrow s}$ and $P_{s\rightarrow a}$ each compose the attention value and output projections. The configurations retain the same encoder and task definitions while testing distinct intermediate designs (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:model-train}

The training protocol supports controlled dual--single comparisons in the plain and RCNN configurations. Within each comparison, the models use the same encoder architecture, intermediate configuration, sarcasm data and fold assignments, and sarcasm-specific hyperparameters. The dual model additionally receives AuthTrain batches, updates shared parameters from both tasks, follows the mixed-task schedule, and uses joint early stopping; the comparison therefore evaluates the complete joint-training procedure rather than any single component.

Training uses a mixed loader that supplies task-homogeneous batches according to a 2:1 MGTD:sarcasm schedule, matching the approximate data-size ratio between AuthTrain and SarcTrain. Let $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ denote parameters updated by both tasks, let $z_s(x)$ and $z_a(x)$ be the representations read by the sarcasm and MGTD heads, and let $W_s$ and $W_a$ denote the two classification heads. The two representations coincide in the plain and RCNN configurations; in the interaction-projection configuration, they are the task-specific $h_s'$ and $h_a'$ defined above. At step $t$, the loss is computed only for the task corresponding to the current batch $B_t$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
\mathcal{L}(B_t)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}_{w}\bigl(W_s z_s(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{SarcTrain},\\[9pt]
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}\bigl(W_a z_a(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{AuthTrain},
\end{cases}$}
\end{equation}

where $\mathrm{CE}_w$ is a pre-specified cost-sensitive cross-entropy for sarcasm (weights are provided in \Appref{app:hyper}). For the class-balanced SarcTrain dataset, the weighted loss assigns a greater penalty to errors on sarcastic examples. Softmax is applied to logits only when probabilities are required. Each task-homogeneous batch updates the active task head and the shared parameters. Unless otherwise stated, binary predictions use a fixed probability threshold of 0.5 rather than a threshold tuned on DualTest.

The encoder uses a lower learning rate than newly added modules. The sarcasm-head rate is fixed across configurations; MGTD-head rates are reported in \Appref{app:hyper}.

Model selection accounts for both tasks. At epoch $e$, the early-stopping signal is the equally weighted sum of the two validation losses, each normalized by its first-epoch value:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stop}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}^{(e)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(1)}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(1)}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$ are the epoch-$e$ validation losses for the sarcasm and MGTD tasks on their respective validation sets. Normalization reduces the influence of differences in task-specific loss scales and learning dynamics.

Five runs are defined by the SarcTrain validation folds: four folds are used for sarcasm training and one for validation, while AuthTrain uses a fixed 90/10 split. Each run reloads the pretrained encoder and randomly initializes new modules and heads. Configurations share fold assignments and nominal seeds, enabling split-matched comparisons but not identical initialization.

DualTest is not included in any training or validation split for either task. Five-run standard deviations combine fold and initialization variation. Wilson intervals are conditional on a fixed final model and cover test-instance, not training or generator, uncertainty (\Tabref{tab:quadrant}; \hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}).

For each configuration used in the subgroup analysis, a final model is trained on all applicable data with a fixed epoch count determined from the five validation runs, an independent seed, and no early stopping. DualTest is not used to train these models or determine their training duration, and the final models are not included in five-run aggregates; \Sref{sec:eval} uses the final RCNN dual-head model (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\section{Experiments and Diagnosis}
\label{sec:exp}

With the sarcasm and MGTD models fixed, we use DualTest first to compare encoder backbones and intermediate configurations, then to measure the change between the joint- and single-task procedures, and finally to partition predictions into source $\times$ sarcasm groups. Aggregate results are five-run means $\pm$ standard deviations; fixed final models support paired instance-level subgroup comparisons and Wilson intervals.

\subsection{Backbone Comparison}
\label{sec:backbone}

We compare dual-head mBERT \citep{devlin2019bert}, XLM-RoBERTa-base \citep{conneau2020xlmr}, DeBERTa-v3-base, and DeBERTa-v3-large \citep{he2023debertav3} under one evaluation protocol, adding the two intermediate variants on the large encoder. Positive-class F1 is computed for the sarcastic class in sarcasm detection and the human class in MGTD over the full DualTest set.

\Tabref{tab:backbone} shows higher mean F1 for the DeBERTa configurations than for mBERT and XLM-R. Among the evaluated configurations, RCNN obtains the highest means, reaching 0.6309 sarcasm F1 and 0.9301 MGTD F1. The two intermediate variants also show smaller standard deviations across the five runs than the plain large model. Because backbone identity, scale, and intermediate representation differ jointly, these results support only configuration-level comparisons. For the separately trained final RCNN model, sarcasm F1 is 0.819 in Chinese and 0.429 in English; prevalence, genre, annotation, and language may all contribute to this difference, so it should not be read as a pure language effect. \Appref{app:results} provides macro-averaged, human-subset, and language-stratified results.

\subsection{Source $\times$ Sarcasm Subgroup Analysis}
\label{sec:quadrant}

The aggregate F1 decrease is highly uneven across source $\times$ sarcasm groups. We therefore examine the four DualTest quadrants separately. Human texts retain dataset sarcasm annotations; for AI texts, the sarcasm labels denote requested styles. Because each group contains only one reference class, we report recall for sarcastic texts and false-positive rate for non-sarcastic texts.

Within each pair, both models use the same all-human sarcasm data; only the dual-head model is additionally trained on MGTD. Their difference therefore measures the complete joint-training procedure. \Tabref{tab:quadrant} reports sarcasm predictions; corresponding MGTD results are in \Appref{app:results}.

False-positive rates on AI $\times$ non-sarcastic instances are 86.3\% for the plain dual-head model, 40.9\% for interaction projection, 34.1\% for RCNN, and 19.3\% for both same-architecture single-head controls; the intermediate rates are therefore more than 45 pp below the plain rate.

On the same AI non-sarcastic texts, 1{,}021 false positives occur only under the plain dual-head model, versus 7 only under its single-head control ($p<.001$). The control receives the same all-human sarcasm supervision but is not jointly trained on MGTD, tying the large increase to the joint-training procedure under comparison. RCNN shows the same direction with a smaller gap. The plain and RCNN models provide the matched contrasts, while interaction projection provides a complementary comparison across configurations.

No analogous extreme subgroup error appears in the MGTD head: its false-positive rate is 6.1\% for plain and 6.6\% for RCNN. Within AI-generated texts, sarcasm AUC remains 0.82--0.88 across models, so the relative ranking of sarcastic and non-sarcastic AI texts is largely preserved. Joint training instead shifts predicted sarcasm probabilities upward, causing many texts prompted to be non-sarcastic to cross the fixed 0.5 threshold. This source-conditioned score shift, rather than a collapse of within-AI ranking ability, characterizes the subgroup failure.

The matched plain comparison makes the concentration especially clear: its non-sarcastic false-positive rate changes by 4.3 pp on human text but by 67.0 pp on AI-generated text.\footnote{Percentage-point differences are computed from unrounded rates and can therefore differ by 0.1 pp from subtracting the rounded values shown in the tables.} The aggregate F1 decrease is therefore driven by a sharply localized source $\times$ class effect rather than a uniform loss across DualTest.

\subsubsection{Representation-Level Diagnostics}
\label{sec:mechanism}

We next ask whether source information in the shared representation is associated with the higher sarcasm scores. A linear probe predicts source from both dual-head (AUC 0.986) and same-architecture single-head (0.974) representations. Source decodability alone therefore does not explain the dual--single gap. In the dual-head model, texts with a higher MGTD-head AI logit also tend to have a higher sarcasm logit in the target subgroup ($\rho=0.519$). After covariance normalization, the source directions estimated from the MGTD head and the probe are also moderately aligned with the sarcasm direction. Together, the logit correlation and directional alignment provide correlational evidence linking source-related structure to the affected sarcasm decisions.

We then modify the frozen representations and recompute predictions. LEACE \citep{belrose2023leace} substantially reduces linear source decodability, lowering source-probe AUC from 0.982 to 0.589, but mainly reallocates errors between source groups. The AI/human non-sarcastic false-positive rates change from 84.8\%/32.8\% to 68.7\%/49.5\%, while their unweighted mean remains nearly unchanged (58.8\% to 59.1\%).

A key control removes a high-variance direction constructed to be orthogonal to the estimated linear source direction. This changes the AI/human false-positive rates to 66.7\%/54.1\% even though measured source decodability remains 0.982; ten sampled random directions show no comparable change. Together, these controls identify a non-random high-variance direction to which source-group errors are sensitive. Combined with the preceding logit correlations and directional alignment, the results support a source-correlated shortcut hypothesis and provide a focused target for further causal analysis (\Appref{app:repr}).

\subsubsection{Fixed-Detector Evaluation on a Second Generator}
\label{sec:crossgen}

We keep the trained detectors fixed and compare the DualTest AI texts generated by \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} with replacements generated by Qwen3.5-9B (Ollama tag \texttt{qwen3.5:9b}) \citep{qwen2026qwen35}. After quality control, the non-sarcastic subsets generated by \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} and Qwen3.5-9B contain 1{,}513 and 1{,}550 outputs. The plain dual-head false-positive rates are 86.3\% and 91.3\%, whereas the corresponding single-head rates are 19.3\% and 7.9\%; the dual--single gap therefore changes from 67.0 to 83.4 pp. On the Qwen3.5-generated subset, the RCNN and interaction-projection rates remain much lower than the plain dual-head rate, at 22.1\% and 29.5\%. The dual--single contrast retains the same direction on Qwen3.5 outputs, and the three dual-head configurations retain the same ordering, although the magnitudes differ (\mbox{Appendix~\ref*{app:c7}}).

\section{Exploratory Comparison of Detector Scores and LLM-Judge Ratings}
\label{sec:eval}

As a downstream test, we compare the RCNN detector's human-source and sarcasm probabilities with source-label-blinded LLM ratings of human-likeness and sarcasm. We evaluate 600 outputs from Qwen3-8B \citep{qwen2025} before and after QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023qlora} fine-tuning on GenTrain. DeepSeek-V3 \citep{deepseek2024v3}, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite \citep{googledeepmind2025gemini25flashlite}, and GPT-5-nano \citep{openai2025gpt5} rate outputs from 180 Chinese topic-comment and 120 English rewriting prompts; language is therefore coupled with generation task. Because predicted human-source probability and perceived human-likeness are distinct constructs, this comparison tests whether classifier scores can serve as generation-evaluation proxies.

Detector probabilities and judge ratings do not move consistently. In Chinese, SFT raises the RCNN detector's sarcasm score from 0.780 to 0.895 but lowers the mean LLM sarcasm rating from 3.99 to 3.80; the detector--judge correlation is $-0.020$ in Chinese and $+0.338$ in English. SFT also raises predicted human-source probabilities in both task--language settings while mean perceived human-likeness ratings decline, yielding a pooled correlation of $-0.212$; weaker within-condition correlations show that this value is driven mainly by the condition-level reversal.

The sarcasm-detector change is setting-dependent ($+0.115$ in Chinese; $-0.124$ in English), whereas SFT receives lower mean sarcasm ratings in all six language $\times$ judge comparisons, five of which remain significant after FDR correction. Detector probabilities can therefore remain useful classification outputs without preserving the ordering or condition-level direction required of style or quality scores.

Individual-text judge agreement is low to moderate; a plain-detector control changes correlations by at most 0.02. The human-source/human-likeness reversal and judge-specific sarcasm results motivate external validation before detector probabilities are reused as quality scores or rewards; \hyperref[app:results]{Appendices~\ref*{app:results}}--\hyperref[app:cases]{\ref*{app:cases}} report full protocols, tests, and cases.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our results identify a source-conditioned negative-transfer pattern in joint MGTD--sarcasm training: relative to a same-architecture single-head control, the plain dual-head model raises the requested-style false-positive rate on AI text prompted to be non-sarcastic by 67.0 pp. Within AI-generated texts, relative ranking remains largely preserved; the subgroup effect is an upward shift in sarcasm scores rather than a collapse of within-source discrimination. The plain dual--single contrast has the same direction in both languages and under fixed-detector evaluation of Qwen3.5-9B outputs, while RCNN and interaction projection yield lower subgroup rates in the evaluated configurations. Representation diagnostics support a source-correlated shortcut hypothesis and identify a focused target for further causal analysis. Predicted human-source probabilities and human-likeness ratings diverge, motivating external validation before detector scores are used as quality metrics or rewards.

\section*{Limitations}

DualTest combines human sarcasm annotations with requested-style sarcasm labels for AI texts, so AI-side false positives are defined relative to requested style rather than independent perceived-sarcasm annotation.

The core dual--single comparisons evaluate the complete joint-training procedure: architecture, sarcasm data, folds, sarcasm-head learning rate, and class weights are shared, whereas MGTD batches, task scheduling, shared updates, and joint early stopping are introduced together. Compute-matched controls could further decompose this effect. Cross-architecture results are configuration-level because capacity and optimization differ, and the cost-sensitive loss can affect absolute rates.

Because oversampling precedes fold assignment, some SarcTrain copies occur in different folds and can appear on both sides of a training--validation split, introducing dependence into checkpoint selection. The affected records are absent from DualTest. Generation failures from \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} leave 80 human texts without generated counterparts. AuthTrain is generated with \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b}; Qwen3.5-9B replaces only DualTest's AI side and therefore provides test-side directional evidence rather than a retraining-based replication.

The fixed-model linear analyses connect source-related structure to behavior but do not isolate a unique causal feature or characterize variation across training seeds. The detector--judge study uses ratings from LLMs rather than humans and couples language with generation task. Future work should test these findings under independent style annotation, matched training budgets, and broader human and cross-generator evaluation.

\section*{Ethical considerations}

All human-authored texts come from publicly released research datasets: iSarcasm, CSC, ToSarcasm, News Headlines, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus. Explicit repository licenses were available for iSarcasm and CSC; for sources without an identified redistribution license, we do not redistribute the human-authored text (\Appref{app:data}). We collect no new user data. We did not conduct any additional audit of the released corpora for personally identifiable information or offensive content.

All AI texts were generated locally using publicly released open-weight models (\texttt{deepseek-r1:8b}, Qwen3.5-9B, and the base and QLoRA-adapted Qwen3-8B). They are used only for training and evaluation and are labeled as AI-generated. No new human participants were recruited; the study analyzes human-authored texts from previously released datasets. The automated evaluation transmitted each evaluation input and generated output, together with rating instructions, to the DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, and GPT-5-nano APIs; it did not transmit dataset metadata or ground-truth source labels.

The detectors were developed for research diagnosis. Their outputs should neither be used as evidence of authorship or misconduct at the individual-text level without human review and domain-specific validation nor be deployed for content moderation or automated sanctions without evaluating out-of-distribution behavior. The class-conditional failure diagnosed here is an example of systematic subgroup error affecting a particular type of generated content.

AI-assisted tools were used to support language editing, structural revision, and formatting. They did not generate the reported experimental outputs or statistics; the authors determined the study design, analysis, and conclusions and take full responsibility for the work.
