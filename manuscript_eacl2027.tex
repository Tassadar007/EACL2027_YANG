\begin{abstract}
A sarcastic utterance can communicate an attitude that differs from what its literal wording suggests. Because recognizing this contrast depends on implicit intent and context, sarcasm is difficult to detect automatically. LLM-generated examples could improve detection by augmenting training data. However, this strategy requires LLMs to produce text that both conveys sarcasm and resembles human writing, so sarcasm and authorship must be evaluated together. Although sarcasm detection and machine-generated text detection (MGTD) are usually studied separately, their interaction in shared representations remains insufficiently understood. MGTD must encode source cues, whereas sarcasm detection should reflect intent rather than source. To test whether any resulting interference is confined to one language, we provide a bilingual diagnosis of joint MGTD--sarcasm training in Chinese and English. We harmonize bilingual resources, generate topic-matched AI text, and build DualTest with source and sarcasm labels, defining AI labels by requested style. Compared with their single-task counterparts, jointly trained models are markedly more likely to misclassify AI-generated texts prompted to be non-sarcastic as sarcastic. Detector-based human-authorship scores and LLM ratings of human-likeness also produce different rankings of the outputs. These findings motivate subgroup evaluation and caution against detector-only assessment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

In a sarcastic sentence, the speaker's attitude or intent may differ from what the literal wording suggests. As a characteristically human form of expression, sarcasm relies on implicit contrast, intent, and context, making it difficult to detect automatically. Detection errors can reverse sentiment predictions and disrupt opinion-mining or content-moderation systems \citep{tay2018reasoning,vidgen2019challenges}. Annotation outcomes also vary with elicitation method and annotator perspective \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,casola2024multipico}, while model behavior is sensitive to domain, style, and language \citep{jang2024csc,casola2024multipico} (\Sref{sec:related-sarcasm}).

One potential response is to augment training data with LLM-generated examples. This strategy is useful only if generated text both conveys the intended sarcasm and resembles human writing; otherwise, source-specific artifacts may become shortcuts. Sarcasm and authorship must therefore be considered together. Human-authored and AI-generated texts can each be sarcastic or non-sarcastic, yielding all four combinations.

Machine-generated text detection (MGTD) imposes a contrasting representational demand: it must distinguish human-written from AI-generated text and thus favors source-predictive representations \citep{guo2023hc3,wang2024m4} (\Sref{sec:related-ai}). These stylistic or distributional cues may overlap with features used for sarcasm detection. The labels do not conflict; the potential conflict lies in how the tasks organize a shared representation. MGTD must encode source, whereas sarcasm detection should reflect intent rather than source. Shared-encoder multi-task learning can suffer task conflict and negative transfer by exploiting information useful for one task but spurious for another \citep{mueller2022taskconflict,hu2022spurious}. Separate models avoid this coupling but forgo efficient encoder sharing, motivating a direct test of whether joint training contaminates sarcasm decisions.

Detector scores also matter downstream because they can become generation rewards. ViSP derives PPO rewards from a sarcasm detector \citep{wang2026visp}. Yet useful generated sarcasm must satisfy two distinct objectives: conveying sarcastic intent and being perceived as human-like. A dual-head detector may appear to provide corresponding signals through sarcasm and authorship probabilities, but authorship probability is not itself a measure of human-likeness. We therefore test whether these detector scores track source-blinded LLM ratings of sarcasm and perceived human-likeness (\Sref{sec:eval}).

To test these risks rather than assume MGTD is a beneficial auxiliary task, we compare same-architecture dual- and single-head models on bilingual Chinese--English data and build DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup analysis. The evaluated joint-training procedure sharply increases the sarcasm false-positive rate on AI text prompted to be non-sarcastic; we call this class-conditional negative transfer. The same pattern persists when the AI test texts are regenerated with Qwen3.5-9B, while the RCNN and interaction-projection configurations show lower rates than the plain configuration.

We then examine the shared representation. Source information is highly decodable and correlates with sarcasm decisions. To connect encoded information to actual decisions beyond probe accuracy \citep{hewitt2019probes,elazar2021amnesic}, we combine probes, task-head correlations, and representation interventions. These analyses identify a non-random high-variance direction to which the subgroup gap is sensitive, supporting a source-correlated shortcut hypothesis and providing a concrete target for causal analysis (\Sref{sec:mechanism}, \Appref{app:repr}).

\paragraph{Contributions.} To our knowledge, we provide the first bilingual diagnostic study of how joint training with MGTD changes sarcasm detection across Chinese and English, together with DualTest for source $\times$ sarcasm analysis. Same-architecture comparisons across models and generators identify class-conditional negative transfer concentrated on AI-generated text prompted to be non-sarcastic; cross-architecture comparisons characterize how its severity varies across the configurations. Representation analyses support the source-correlated shortcut hypothesis and define concrete targets for causal analysis. Finally, an exploratory detector--judge comparison illustrates a downstream risk of using detector scores to evaluate generated sarcasm and perceived human-likeness.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Sarcasm Detection and Multilingual Resources}
\label{sec:related-sarcasm}

Feature-engineered methods \citep{riloff2013sarcasm}, neural sequence models \citep{ghosh2016fracking,tay2018reasoning}, and pretrained language models \citep{khatri2020,du2022pali} have each advanced sarcasm detection. The task remains difficult in multilingual and class-imbalanced settings, especially because datasets differ in whether labels reflect intended sarcasm or annotator-perceived sarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval,casola2024multipico}. Although scores are not directly comparable across datasets, languages, modalities, or evaluation setups, they illustrate substantial benchmark variability. On the English iSarcasmEval benchmark \citep{abufarha2022isarcasmeval}, the top two systems report positive-class F1 of 0.605 and 0.569; across MultiPICo languages \citep{casola2024multipico}, zero-shot ChatGPT positive-class F1 ranges from 0.152 to 0.567. On the bilingual multimodal SarcNet benchmark \citep{yue2024sarcnet}, a text-only XLM-R baseline reaches 0.734 F1.

RCNNs combine recurrent context with convolutional pooling for text classification \citep{lai2015rcnn} and have also been evaluated for multilingual sarcasm detection \citep{yang2024influence}; we therefore include an RCNN intermediate configuration in \Sref{sec:model-arch}.

The benchmark results summarized above come from different datasets, so \Sref{sec:backbone} provides directly comparable mBERT dual-head baselines on DualTest. None of the sarcasm resources reviewed above provides both sarcasm and human-versus-AI source labels. DualTest supplies both labels in a bilingual Chinese--English setting.

The Chinese and English source datasets differ in domain, annotation procedure, and class balance, so bilinguality introduces additional distributional variation. We treat language as a robustness and stratification dimension rather than as the primary transfer objective: the study asks whether the subgroup failure is confined to one language, not whether knowledge transfers across languages. We therefore report language-stratified results in addition to pooled metrics.

\subsection{Machine-Generated Text Detection and Authorship Verification}
\label{sec:related-ai}

MGTD comprises zero-shot model-likelihood methods, such as probability-curvature detectors \citep{mitchell2023detectgpt}, and discriminative models trained on paired human--AI corpora \citep[HC3;][]{guo2023hc3}. Generalization is a central difficulty: M4 \citep{wang2024m4} provides a benchmark across generators, domains, and languages and shows that detectors struggle on unseen domains or LLMs, while RAID \citep{dugan2024raid} stress-tests detectors under unseen generators, decoding variations, and adversarial attacks. This generalization gap is consistent with reliance on source-predictive regularities that are partly generator- or domain-specific; such cues are relevant here because they may enter a representation shared with sarcasm detection. \citet{bevendorff2025} distinguish closed-set authorship attribution from open-set authorship verification and argue that realistic LLM detection is better cast as the latter. Our binary MGTD setup is narrower: we use it as a controlled source-classification auxiliary task, not as a measure of text quality.

In our literature search, we found no work that jointly trains sarcasm detection and MGTD with a shared encoder.\footnote{Searches conducted in May 2026 and updated in July 2026 across Semantic Scholar, arXiv, ACL Anthology, and Google Scholar, using keyword combinations including ``sarcasm detection,'' ``machine-generated text detection,'' ``AI-generated text detection,'' ``multi-task learning,'' and ``joint detection.''} This gap motivates our study because source information has different roles in the two tasks: it is the target of MGTD but should not by itself determine a sarcasm detector's prediction. Our study tests whether a shared representation preserves that distinction.

\subsection{Multi-Task Negative Transfer, Shortcut Learning, and Shared-Encoder Mitigation}
\label{sec:related-transfer}

In multi-task learning, auxiliary tasks are not always beneficial: transfer depends on task relatedness and alignment between their data distributions \citep{zhang2023survey,wu2020understanding}, while gradient conflict is a central optimization challenge that can contribute to negative transfer \citep{yu2020gradientsurgery,liu2021cagrad}. \citet{mueller2022taskconflict} provide a relevant NLP precedent through matched single- and multi-task comparisons. They find similar negative-transfer patterns in canonical and text-to-text systems and note that models may infer task identity from input distributions, a strategy that may not transfer reliably out of distribution. \citet{he2021stemcell} and \citet{li2023functional} further analyze functional specialization in multi-task Transformer encoders. Shortcut learning offers a complementary reading: models may exploit features that are predictive in the training distribution yet fail under distribution shift \citep{geirhos2020shortcut,ye2024spurious}. In MTL, shared representations can increase reliance on spurious correlations \citep{hu2022spurious,chai2025spurious}. Related multi-dataset work shows that adding data can introduce spurious correlations and reduce worst-group performance \citep{compton2023morisless}.

Shared-encoder mitigation either isolates task-specific parameters \citep{pfeiffer2021adapterfusion,pfeiffer2020madx,mahabadi2021hyperformer,stickland2019pals} or modifies information flow within a shared computation graph \citep{wu2019sifted,pilault2021camtl}. Our analysis is closer to the second design direction: we compare a no-module baseline with two intermediate-representation variants and test whether degradation changes across source $\times$ sarcasm subgroups (\Sref{sec:quadrant}).

\subsection{Misalignment in Detector-Based Evaluation and Rewards}
\label{sec:related-eval}

The representational conflict studied above may also matter when detector outputs are reused beyond classification, particularly as evaluation or reward signals for generated text. Automated scorers, including LLM judges, are increasingly used to evaluate generated text \citep{zheng2023judging}. Model-based scores are also optimized as rewards, but overoptimization can increase a proxy score while degrading a gold-standard measure \citep{gao2023overoptimization}. A detector can achieve useful classification accuracy without its continuous output being calibrated as a measure of style strength or generation quality. Multimodal sarcasm generation may face this risk: ViSP \citep{wang2026visp} uses the DIP sarcasm detector \citep{wen2023dip} as a PPO reward \citep{schulman2017ppo}. As an exploratory downstream analysis, \hyperref[sec:eval]{Section~\ref*{sec:eval}} compares detector scores for human authorship with separately obtained, source-blinded LLM ratings of perceived human-likeness.

\section{Dataset Construction}
\label{sec:data}

Testing for interference requires separate task supervision and evaluation labels for authorship and sarcasm. Here ``source'' denotes human-versus-AI authorship; ``corpus'' denotes an input dataset. We combine iSarcasm \citep{oprea2020isarcasm,abufarha2022isarcasmeval}, CSC \citep{jang2024csc,jang2023intended}, ToSarcasm \citep{liang2022tosarcasm}, News Headlines v2 \citep{misra2023headlines,misra2021sculpting}, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus \citep{zhu2022corpus}. None of these datasets provides both labels. Mapping their annotations to a common binary sarcasm label yields 44{,}862 pooled instances (approximately 87\% English), from which we construct three training sets and DualTest (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Sarcasm classification training set, SarcTrain (10{,}000, all human).} Each language $\times$ label bucket contains 2{,}500 instances. Three buckets are downsampled; the Chinese sarcastic bucket starts from 2{,}396 records and adds 104 oversampled records. The resulting set is organized into five folds.

\paragraph{MGTD training set, AuthTrain (19{,}995).} AuthTrain draws 9{,}999 approximately language-balanced human seeds from SarcTrain (5{,}000 English / 4{,}999 Chinese) after seed-level generation quality control excludes one Chinese seed. The Ollama model tagged \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} \citep{deepseek2025r1,ollama2025deepseek8b} produces 9{,}996 retained AI counterparts; three selected human seeds remain unmatched, giving 99.97\% pairing coverage. \Appref{app:data} details the exclusions.

We use four language $\times$ style templates, with each seed's label determining the requested style. Authorship and requested-style labels form two separately controlled, approximately balanced factors, enabling source $\times$ sarcasm subgroup analysis (\Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Generation training set, GenTrain (2{,}428; used in \Sref{sec:eval}).} From 5{,}257 eligible positive-sarcasm records in iSarcasm and ToSarcasm, we sample 2{,}103 records (40\%; 1{,}214 English and 889 Chinese). Chinese oversampling adds 325 records, yielding 1{,}214 per language. The resulting set contains 374 rewriting, 839 response, and 1{,}215 comment instances and serves as the instruction-tuning data for supervised fine-tuning (SFT; \Appref{app:data}).

\paragraph{Diagnostic test set, DualTest (4{,}664).} The human subset combines official iSarcasm (1{,}400 English) and ToSarcasm (972 Chinese) test sets. After generation quality control, AI counterparts remain for 2{,}292 of 2{,}372 texts (96.6\% coverage). Human texts retain their annotations; for AI texts, the sarcasm label records the prompted condition rather than an independently annotated judgment of perceived sarcasm.

The four source $\times$ sarcasm groups contain 823 human-sarcastic, 1{,}549 human-non-sarcastic, 779 AI-sarcastic, and 1{,}513 AI-non-sarcastic instances. \Appref{app:data} gives the language $\times$ source $\times$ sarcasm distribution.

\section{Dual-Head Detection Model}
\label{sec:model}

\subsection{Tasks and Architecture}
\label{sec:model-arch}

Given a Chinese or English input text $x$, the model produces two separate, non-mutually-exclusive binary predictions: whether $x$ is labeled sarcastic and whether its source label is human or AI. Any combination of the two labels is valid. The tasks share a single DeBERTa-v3-large encoder \citep{he2023debertav3}, allowing us to test how incorporating MGTD into joint training changes the representation read by the sarcasm head. Encoder outputs are aggregated by an intermediate module and then passed to two task-specific classification heads; \Figref{fig:architecture} shows the RCNN configuration.

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_architecture.pdf}
  \caption{RCNN dual-head configuration: a shared DeBERTa-v3-large encoder, an RCNN intermediate module (dashed), and two task-specific heads. The comparisons in \Sref{sec:exp} remove this component in the plain configuration or replace it with a task-branch interaction-projection module, while retaining the same encoder backbone and task definitions (\Appref{app:hyper}).}
  \label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

The RCNN configuration uses BiLSTM $+$ CNN as its intermediate module. A single-layer bidirectional LSTM with hidden size 256 per direction produces a 512-dimensional output at each token position. We concatenate this output with the 1{,}024-dimensional encoder output, then apply three groups of one-dimensional convolutions with kernel widths 3/4/5 and 128 channels each. Global max pooling produces a 384-dimensional vector $z$. After dropout, $z$ is passed to the two heads, yielding a DeBERTa-based dual-head RCNN configuration.

To test the intermediate layer's role, \Sref{sec:exp} compares two variants. The plain model sends \texttt{[CLS]} directly to both heads. The interaction-projection model forms two learned 256-dimensional projections, $h_s$ and $h_a$, and updates them as $h_s'=h_s+P_{a\rightarrow s}(h_a)$ and $h_a'=h_a+P_{s\rightarrow a}(h_s)$. Because each branch supplies only one key--value vector, every attention head assigns a weight of one to that vector; the module therefore reduces to residual cross-branch affine projections, where $P_{a\rightarrow s}$ and $P_{s\rightarrow a}$ each compose the attention value and output projections. The configurations retain the same encoder and task definitions while testing distinct intermediate-representation designs (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\subsection{Training Protocol}
\label{sec:model-train}

The training protocol supports controlled dual--single comparisons in the plain and RCNN configurations. Within each comparison, the models use the same encoder architecture, intermediate configuration, sarcasm data and fold assignments, and sarcasm-specific hyperparameters. The dual model additionally receives AuthTrain batches, updates shared parameters from both tasks, follows the mixed-task schedule, and uses joint early stopping; the comparison therefore evaluates the complete joint-training procedure rather than any single component.

Training uses a mixed loader that supplies task-homogeneous batches according to a 2:1 MGTD:sarcasm schedule, matching the approximate data-size ratio between AuthTrain and SarcTrain. Let $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ denote parameters updated by both tasks, let $z_s(x)$ and $z_a(x)$ be the representations read by the sarcasm and MGTD heads, and let $W_s$ and $W_a$ denote the two classification heads. The two representations coincide in the plain and RCNN configurations; in the interaction-projection configuration, they are the task-specific $h_s'$ and $h_a'$ defined above. At step $t$, the loss is computed only for the task corresponding to the current batch $B_t$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\resizebox{\columnwidth}{!}{$\displaystyle
\mathcal{L}(B_t)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}_{w}\bigl(W_s z_s(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{SarcTrain},\\[9pt]
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}\bigl(W_a z_a(x), y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{AuthTrain},
\end{cases}$}
\end{equation}

where $\mathrm{CE}_w$ is a pre-specified cost-sensitive cross-entropy for sarcasm (weights are provided in \Appref{app:hyper}). For the class-balanced SarcTrain dataset, the weighted loss assigns a greater penalty to errors on sarcastic examples. Softmax is applied to logits only when probabilities are required. The gradient flow follows from the equation: on sarcasm batches, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_a = 0$ (symmetrically, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_s = 0$ on MGTD batches), whereas $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ receives gradients from both types of batches. Unless otherwise stated, binary predictions use a fixed probability threshold of 0.5 rather than a threshold tuned on DualTest.

The encoder uses a lower learning rate than newly added modules. The sarcasm-head rate is fixed across configurations; MGTD-head rates are reported in \Appref{app:hyper}.

Model selection accounts for both tasks. At epoch $e$, the early-stopping signal is the equally weighted sum of the two validation losses, each normalized by its first-epoch value:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:stop}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}^{(e)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(1)}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(1)}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$ are the epoch-$e$ validation losses for the sarcasm and MGTD tasks on their respective validation sets. Normalization reduces the influence of differences in task-specific loss scales and learning dynamics.

Five runs are defined by the SarcTrain validation folds: four folds are used for sarcasm training and one for validation, while AuthTrain uses a fixed 90/10 split. Each run reloads the pretrained encoder and randomly initializes new modules and heads. Configurations share fold assignments and nominal seeds, enabling split-matched comparisons but not identical initialization.

DualTest is not included in any training or validation split for either task. Five-run standard deviations combine fold and initialization variation. Wilson intervals are conditional on a fixed final model and cover test-instance, not training or generator, uncertainty (\Tabref{tab:quadrant}; \hyperref[app:c7]{Appendix~C.7}).

For each configuration used in the subgroup analysis, a final model is trained on all applicable data with a fixed epoch count determined from the five validation runs, an independent seed, and no early stopping. DualTest is not used to train these models or determine their training duration, and the final models are not included in five-run aggregates; \Sref{sec:eval} uses the final RCNN dual-head model (\Appref{app:hyper}).

\section{Experiments and Diagnosis}
\label{sec:exp}

With the sarcasm and MGTD models fixed, we use DualTest first to compare encoder backbones and intermediate configurations, then to measure the change between the joint- and single-task procedures, and finally to partition predictions into source $\times$ sarcasm groups. Aggregate results are five-run means $\pm$ standard deviations; fixed final models support paired instance-level subgroup comparisons and Wilson intervals.

\subsection{Backbone Comparison}
\label{sec:backbone}

We compare dual-head mBERT \citep{devlin2019bert}, XLM-RoBERTa-base \citep{conneau2020xlmr}, DeBERTa-v3-base, and DeBERTa-v3-large \citep{he2023debertav3} under one evaluation protocol, adding the two intermediate variants on the large encoder. Positive-class F1 is computed for the sarcastic class in sarcasm detection and the human class in MGTD over the full DualTest set.

\Tabref{tab:backbone} shows higher mean F1 for the DeBERTa configurations than for mBERT and XLM-R. The RCNN configuration has the highest means, reaching 0.6309 sarcasm F1 and 0.9301 MGTD F1. Across the five runs, the two intermediate variants also have 34--47\% lower standard deviations than the plain large model, providing configuration-level evidence of lower run-to-run variation. Because backbone identity, scale, and intermediate representation differ jointly, these results support only configuration-level comparisons. For the separately trained final RCNN model, sarcasm F1 is 0.819 in Chinese and 0.429 in English; prevalence, genre, annotation, and language may all contribute to this difference, so it should not be read as a pure language effect. \Appref{app:results} provides macro-averaged, human-subset, and language-stratified results.

\subsection{Source $\times$ Sarcasm Subgroup Analysis}
\label{sec:quadrant}

An overall F1 score can hide a failure concentrated in one type of text. We therefore divide DualTest into four groups defined by source and sarcasm label. Human labels are dataset annotations, whereas AI labels record the prompted condition. Because each group contains only one reference class, we report recall for sarcastic texts and false-positive rate for non-sarcastic texts.

Their difference therefore measures the complete joint-training procedure. \Tabref{tab:quadrant} reports sarcasm predictions; corresponding MGTD results are in \Appref{app:results}.

False-positive rates on AI $\times$ non-sarcastic instances are 86.3\% for the plain dual-head model, 40.9\% for interaction projection, 34.1\% for RCNN, and 19.3\% for both same-architecture single-head controls; the intermediate rates are therefore more than 45 pp below the plain rate.

On the same AI non-sarcastic texts, 1{,}021 false positives occur only under the plain dual-head model, versus 7 only under its single-head control ($p<.001$). The control receives the same all-human sarcasm supervision but is not jointly trained on MGTD, tying the large increase to the joint-training procedure under comparison. RCNN shows the same direction with a smaller gap. The plain and RCNN models provide the matched contrasts, while interaction projection adds complementary configuration-level evidence.

No analogous extreme subgroup error appears in the MGTD head: its false-positive rate is 6.1\% for plain and 6.6\% for RCNN. Within AI-generated texts, sarcasm AUC remains 0.82--0.88 across models, so the relative ranking of sarcastic and non-sarcastic AI texts is largely preserved. Joint training instead shifts predicted sarcasm probabilities upward, causing many texts prompted to be non-sarcastic to cross the fixed 0.5 threshold. The failure therefore takes the form of an AI-associated score increase in this subgroup, not a complete loss of within-AI ranking ability.

The matched plain comparison makes the concentration especially clear: its non-sarcastic false-positive rate changes by 4.2 pp on human text but by 67.0 pp on AI-generated text. The aggregate F1 decrease is therefore driven by a sharply localized source $\times$ class effect rather than a uniform loss across DualTest.

\subsubsection{Mechanism Hypothesis and Representation-Level Diagnostics}
\label{sec:mechanism}

We next ask whether source information in the shared representation is associated with the higher sarcasm scores. A linear probe predicts source from both dual-head (AUC 0.986) and same-architecture single-head (0.974) representations. The comparable single-head AUC shows that source decodability is a background property shared by both models. In the dual-head model, texts with a higher MGTD-head AI logit also tend to have a higher sarcasm logit in the target subgroup ($\rho=0.519$). After covariance normalization, the source directions estimated from the MGTD head and the probe are also moderately aligned with the sarcasm direction. Together, the logit correlation and directional alignment provide correlational evidence linking source-related structure to the affected sarcasm decisions.

We then modify the frozen representations and recompute predictions. LEACE \citep{belrose2023leace}, which reduces linearly decodable source information, lowers source-probe AUC from 0.982 to 0.589. It also changes the AI/human non-sarcastic false-positive rates from 84.8\%/32.8\% to 68.7\%/49.5\%. The nearly unchanged unweighted mean (58.8\% to 59.1\%) indicates that LEACE reallocates errors between source groups without reducing their unweighted mean.

A key control removes a high-variance direction constructed to be orthogonal to the estimated linear source direction. This changes the AI/human false-positive rates to 66.7\%/54.1\% even though measured source decodability remains 0.982; ten sampled random directions show no comparable change. Together, these controls show prediction sensitivity to a non-random high-variance direction. The source-correlated shortcut hypothesis therefore provides a focused mechanism for further analysis, with the semantic content of the effective direction as the next causal target (\Appref{app:repr}).

\subsubsection{Cross-Generator Replication}
\label{sec:crossgen}

We keep the trained detectors fixed and compare the DualTest AI texts generated by \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} with replacements generated by Qwen3.5-9B (Ollama tag \texttt{qwen3.5:9b}) \citep{qwen2026qwen35}. After quality control, the non-sarcastic subsets generated by \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} and Qwen3.5-9B contain 1{,}513 and 1{,}550 outputs. The plain dual-head false-positive rates are 86.3\% and 91.3\%, whereas the corresponding single-head rates are 19.3\% and 7.9\%; the dual--single gap therefore changes from 67.0 to 83.4 pp. On the Qwen3.5-generated subset, the RCNN and interaction-projection rates remain much lower than the plain dual-head rate, at 22.1\% and 29.5\%. The gap has the same direction and the configurations have the same ordering with Qwen3.5, although the magnitudes differ (\mbox{Appendix~\ref*{app:c7}}).

\section{Exploratory Comparison of Detector Scores and LLM-Judge Ratings}
\label{sec:eval}

As a downstream test, we compare the RCNN detector's human-authorship and sarcasm probabilities with source-blinded LLM ratings of human-likeness and sarcasm. We evaluate 600 outputs from Qwen3-8B \citep{qwen2025} before and after QLoRA \citep{dettmers2023qlora} fine-tuning on GenTrain. DeepSeek-V3 \citep{deepseek2024v3}, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite \citep{googledeepmind2025gemini25flashlite}, and GPT-5-nano \citep{openai2025gpt5} rate outputs from 180 Chinese topic-comment and 120 English rewriting prompts; language is therefore coupled with generation task. Because human-authorship probability and perceived human-likeness are distinct constructs, this comparison tests whether classifier scores can serve as generation-evaluation proxies.

Detector probabilities and judge ratings do not move consistently. In Chinese, SFT raises the RCNN detector's sarcasm score from 0.780 to 0.895 but lowers the mean LLM sarcasm rating from 3.99 to 3.80; the detector--judge correlation is $-0.020$ in Chinese and $+0.338$ in English. SFT also raises human-authorship probabilities in both task--language settings while mean perceived human-likeness ratings decline, yielding a pooled correlation of $-0.212$; weaker within-condition correlations show that this value is driven mainly by the condition-level reversal.

The sarcasm-detector change is setting-dependent ($+0.115$ in Chinese; $-0.124$ in English), whereas SFT receives lower mean sarcasm ratings in all six language $\times$ judge comparisons, five remaining significant after FDR correction. Detector probabilities can therefore remain useful classification outputs without preserving the ordering or condition-level direction required of style or quality scores.

Individual-text judge agreement is low to moderate; a plain-detector control changes correlations by at most 0.02. The human-authorship/human-likeness reversal and judge-specific sarcasm results motivate external validation before detector probabilities are reused as quality scores or rewards; \hyperref[app:results]{Appendices~\ref*{app:results}}--\hyperref[app:cases]{\ref*{app:cases}} report full protocols, tests, and cases.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Our results expose a source-related shortcut risk in joint MGTD--sarcasm training: relative to a same-architecture single-head control, the plain dual-head model raises the false-positive rate on AI text prompted to be non-sarcastic by 67.0 pp. The pattern holds across languages and with Qwen3.5-9B, while RCNN and interaction projection yield lower rates. Representation interventions show that source-related and other high-variance directions shape source-group errors, providing targets for causal analysis. Human-authorship probabilities and human-likeness ratings diverge, requiring external validation of detector scores by source and class before use as quality metrics or rewards.

\section*{Limitations}

DualTest combines human sarcasm annotations with instruction-conditioned labels for AI texts, so AI-side false positives are defined relative to requested style rather than independent perceived-sarcasm annotation. Generation failures from \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b} leave 80 human texts without generated counterparts. Because oversampling precedes fold assignment, some SarcTrain copies occur in different folds and can therefore appear on both sides of a training--validation split, introducing dependence into checkpoint selection. The affected records are absent from DualTest.

The core dual--single comparisons evaluate the complete joint-training procedure: architecture, sarcasm data, folds, sarcasm-head learning rate, and class weights are shared, whereas MGTD batches, task scheduling, shared updates, and joint early stopping are introduced together. Compute-matched controls could further decompose this effect. Cross-architecture results are configuration-level because capacity and optimization differ, and the cost-sensitive loss can affect absolute rates. AuthTrain is generated with \texttt{deepseek-r1:8b}; Qwen3.5-9B replaces only DualTest's AI side and therefore provides a directional replication on test data from a second generator rather than a retraining-based replication.

The fixed-model linear analyses connect source-related structure to behavior but do not isolate a unique causal feature or characterize variation across training seeds. The detector--judge study uses ratings from LLMs rather than humans and couples language with generation task. These boundaries motivate independent style annotation, matched-budget controls, cross-seed mechanism tests, and broader generator and human-evaluation studies.

\section*{Ethical considerations}

All human-authored texts come from publicly released research datasets: iSarcasm, CSC, ToSarcasm, News Headlines, and the Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus. Explicit repository licenses were available for iSarcasm and CSC; for sources without an identified redistribution license, we do not redistribute the human-authored text (\Appref{app:data}). We collect no new user data. We did not conduct any additional audit of the released corpora for personally identifiable information or offensive content.

All AI texts were generated locally using publicly released open-weight models (\texttt{deepseek-r1:8b}, Qwen3.5-9B, and the base and QLoRA-adapted Qwen3-8B). They are used only for training and evaluation and are labeled as AI-generated. No new human participants were recruited; the study analyzes human-authored texts from previously released datasets. The automated evaluation transmitted each evaluation input and generated output, together with rating instructions, to the DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, and GPT-5-nano APIs; it did not transmit dataset metadata or ground-truth source labels.

The detectors were developed for research diagnosis. Their outputs should neither be used as evidence of authorship or misconduct at the individual-text level without human review and domain-specific validation nor be deployed for content moderation or automated sanctions without evaluating out-of-distribution behavior. The class-conditional failure diagnosed here is an example of systematic subgroup error affecting a particular type of generated content.

AI-assisted tools were used for grammar correction, wording suggestions, and formatting. They did not generate the reported experimental outputs or statistics; the authors determined the study design, analysis, and conclusions and take full responsibility for the work.
