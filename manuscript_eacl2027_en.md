# When Joint Training with Machine-Generated Text Detection Can Distort Sarcasm Detection: A Bilingual Diagnosis of Class-Conditional Negative Transfer

## Abstract <!-- budget: ~0.35 pages -->

Sarcasm is non-literal language whose intended meaning may diverge from apparent sentiment, complicating detection in online text. As LLM-generated content appears alongside human writing, systems may need to judge both sarcasm and machine authorship. These judgments also interact in generation, where detector scores may be treated as measures of sarcastic intent and human authorship. Although sarcasm detection and machine-generated text detection (MGTD) are studied separately, their interaction in shared representations remains insufficiently understood. MGTD may promote source-predictive stylistic or distributional cues, whereas sarcasm detection should reflect pragmatic intent rather than source. We conduct, to our knowledge, the first bilingual diagnosis of how MGTD supervision affects sarcasm detection across Chinese and English. We harmonize bilingual resources, generate topic-matched AI texts, and build DualTest with source and sarcasm labels. Architecturally matched joint and single-task models reveal class-conditional negative transfer concentrated on AI-generated text prompted to be non-sarcastic. This pattern persists with a second generator and is attenuated by RCNN and interaction-projection modules. An exploratory comparison shows that detector scores for human authorship and LLM ratings of perceived human-likeness rank generated outputs differently. These findings motivate subgroup evaluation and caution against detector-only assessment.

## 1 Introduction <!-- budget: ~1 page -->

Sarcasm is a prototypical form of non-literal expression: speakers often convey an intended negative meaning through superficially positive wording. Because stable lexical cues are lacking, sarcasm detection has long depended on context, intent, and background knowledge, and annotation agreement is relatively low; multilingual settings amplify these difficulties further. The proliferation of LLMs introduces an additional source dimension: sarcastic social-media text may be written either by a human or generated by a model under instruction. In real-world deployment, “is this sarcastic?” and “was this written by a human?” are therefore no longer entirely independent questions, but judgments that may be required simultaneously of the same input text. The coupling also appears on the generation side: detector scores are already used in practice as evaluation and even reward signals for sarcasm generation (e.g., ViSP; Wang et al., 2026; §2.4), and judging whether generated sarcasm reads as human-written itself involves both dimensions at once. Yet existing work largely treats sentiment/rhetorical analysis, MGTD, and content-safety analysis as separate problems separately, and research that unifies sarcasm detection and MGTD as a joint multi-task learning problem remains absent (see §2.2 for the search protocol).

This paper investigates shared-representation risk in joint detection. When a single backbone serves both source identification and semantic judgment, supervision for one task may alter the other's behavior on particular subgroups. We diagnose class-conditional negative transfer: after MGTD training is added, the sarcasm head sharply overpredicts sarcasm on AI-generated text prompted to be non-sarcastic. The increase is induced by the complete joint-training procedure, its direction persists when the AI test side is regenerated by a second generator, and two intermediate configurations reduce its observed severity. Representation diagnostics show that source is highly decodable and correlated with sarcasm decisions, while the intervention effect is also reproduced by removing a high-variance direction orthogonal to the estimated linear source direction. We therefore treat a source-correlated shortcut as a supported but bounded mechanism hypothesis: the effective directions' semantic content and source-specific causal role remain unresolved.

**Contributions.** Following the structure of the paper, our contributions are fourfold:

- **A bilingual dataset suite and dual-head diagnostic setting for joint detection (§3–§4).** Four bilingual datasets: SarcTrain (10,000 sarcasm-labeled human texts), AuthTrain (19,995; 9,996 retained AI counterparts for 9,999 human seeds), GenTrain (2,428 generation instructions), and the diagnostic test set DualTest (4,664), whose instances carry both `is_sarcastic` and `is_human` labels and support class-conditional analysis over the four “source × sarcasm” quadrants. To the best of our knowledge, DualTest is the first dual-label stratified test set for sarcasm evaluation, and the dual-head model built on it is the first to formulate the two tasks as a joint multi-task problem (§2.2).
- **Behavioral diagnosis of class-conditional negative transfer (§5).** The plain dual-head model misclassifies 86.3% of AI-generated non-sarcastic texts as sarcastic; a single-head model with the same sarcasm supervision misclassifies only 19.3%. The increase is induced by the complete joint-training procedure, its direction persists with a second generator, and two intermediate configurations reduce its observed severity.
- **Representation-level characterization of the failure and boundaries on mechanism attribution (§5.3.2).** Source information is highly decodable from the sarcasm representation and correlated with sarcasm decisions, yet concept erasure and an orthogonal high-variance control show that the intervention effect is not specific to the source concept. The results support a source-correlated shortcut hypothesis while leaving the directions' semantic content and source-specific causal role unresolved.
- **An exploratory detector–judge comparison (§6).** Human-authorship probabilities and LLM ratings of perceived human-likeness rank generated outputs differently, and sarcasm-score agreement varies by language. This downstream analysis shows why detector probabilities require external validation before use as generation-quality or reward signals.

## 2 Related Work <!-- budget: ~0.95 pages -->

### 2.1 Sarcasm Detection and Multilingual Resources

Feature-engineered methods (Riloff et al., 2013), neural sequence models (Ghosh & Veale, 2016; Tay et al., 2018), and pretrained language models (Khatri & P, 2020; Han et al., 2022) have each advanced sarcasm detection. The task remains difficult in multilingual and class-imbalanced settings, especially when labels target intended rather than perceived sarcasm. Although scores are not directly comparable across datasets, languages, modalities, or evaluation setups, they illustrate substantial benchmark variability: the top two English systems on iSarcasmEval (Abu Farha et al., 2022) report positive-class F1 of 0.605 and 0.569; zero-shot ChatGPT positive-class F1 ranges from 0.152 to 0.567 across MultiPICo languages (Casola et al., 2024); and a text-only XLM-R baseline reaches 0.734 F1 on the bilingual multimodal SarcNet benchmark (Yao et al., 2024). Yang and Ikeda (2024), building on their earlier multilingual mBERT/BiLSTM study (Yang and Ikeda, 2023), compared mBERT-RCNN (mBERT with the recurrent convolutional architecture of Lai et al., 2015) on mixed English–Arabic and English–Chinese training sets and reported improved sarcasm-detection performance on two English test sets. We therefore include RCNN among the intermediate architectures examined in §4.1. Because these benchmark results come from different datasets, §5.1 provides directly comparable mBERT dual-head baselines on DualTest. We use a bilingual Chinese–English setting to test whether the subgroup failure is confined to one language, rather than treating cross-language transfer as the primary objective.

### 2.2 Machine-Generated Text Detection and Authorship Verification

Machine-generated text detection (MGTD) uses zero-shot/perplexity-based detectors (Mitchell et al., 2023) and discriminative models trained on paired human–AI corpora (HC3; Guo et al., 2023); generalization is the recognized central challenge, motivating M4 (Wang et al., 2024) for black-box detection across generators, domains, and languages and RAID (Dugan et al., 2024) for robust detector evaluation. Conceptually, Bevendorff et al. (2025) frame the problem as authorship verification rather than quality assessment; we treat MGTD as an auxiliary task within a shared encoder. Prior work treats sarcasm detection and MGTD as unrelated problems: existing multi-task sarcasm research mainly combines sarcasm with sentiment, stance, or emotion, and to the best of our knowledge no work has studied the two as a joint multi-task problem.[^search] How a shared encoder behaves when both judgments are learned jointly is therefore an open question and the starting point of this paper.

[^search]: The search was conducted in May 2026 across Semantic Scholar, arXiv, ACL Anthology, and Google Scholar, using keyword combinations including “sarcasm detection,” “AI-generated text detection,” “authorship detection,” “multi-task learning,” and “joint detection.”

### 2.3 Multi-Task Negative Transfer, Shortcut Learning, and Shared-Encoder Mitigation

We use negative transfer and shortcut learning as explanatory frameworks for a previously unstudied task combination. In multi-task learning, auxiliary tasks are not always beneficial: transfer direction depends on task relatedness and data geometry (Zhang et al., 2023; Wu, Zhang, & Ré, 2020), and conflicting gradients can cause negative transfer (Yu et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2021). Mueller et al. (2022) provide the closest NLP precedent, showing through single-/multi-task contrasts that models may infer task identity from the input distribution and destabilize on out-of-distribution inputs (see also He & Choi, 2021; Li et al., 2023, on internal mechanisms of multi-task models). Shortcut learning is complementary: models may exploit features predictive in the training distribution but failing on specific subgroups (Geirhos et al., 2020; Ye et al., 2024), which in multi-task settings appears as increased absorption of spurious correlations (Hu et al., 2022b; Chai & Wang, 2025) and subgroup-stratified failures (Compton et al., 2023). Shared-encoder mitigation forms a continuum from task-specific parameter isolation (AdapterFusion, Pfeiffer et al., 2021; MAD-X, Pfeiffer et al., 2020; HyperFormer++, Karimi Mahabadi et al., 2021; PALs, Stickland & Murray, 2019) to gating, attention, or intermediate modules that filter interference in shared representations (Wu et al., 2019; CA-MTL, Pilault et al., 2021); our intermediate-layer analysis sits nearer the latter end. We connect these lines: conditional stratification exposes a subgroup-specific failure induced by joint training, and controlled comparisons of three intermediate structures test the mitigation hypothesis (§5.3).

### 2.4 Misalignment in Detector-Based Evaluation and Rewards

Beyond the main line of failure diagnosis, dual-head detector outputs might also be used as generation-evaluation or reward signals; this section gives the background for the supplementary analysis in §6. LLM-as-a-judge (Zheng et al., 2023) is widely used for generation evaluation, and using automated scorers as rewards risks reward hacking in the Goodhart sense. The practice has entered sarcasm generation: ViSP (Wang et al., 2026) uses scores from the sarcasm detector DIP (Wen et al., 2023) as a PPO reward, its data filtering and parts of its quality analysis rely on the same detector, and its authors list “performance depends on the quality of the external evaluator” as their primary limitation. Since MGTD targets verification rather than quality assessment (§2.2), agreement between detector scores and perceived quality requires a direct test; §6 provides it and analyzes the systematic directional bias that appears when an MGTD detector is used as a generation-evaluation signal.

## 3 Dataset Construction <!-- budget: ~0.9 pages -->

We begin with five heterogeneous sources (iSarcasm: Oprea & Magdy, 2020 / Abu Farha et al., 2022; CSC: Jang & Frassinelli, 2024 / Jang et al., 2023; ToSarcasm: Liang et al., 2022; News Headlines v2: Misra & Arora, 2023 / Misra & Grover, 2021; Open Chinese Internet Sarcasm Corpus: Zhu, 2022; 44,862 instances after standardized merging, approximately 87% English) and obtain three training sets and one diagnostic test set through a three-stage pipeline. Source-level details, annotation methods, and quality checks are provided in Appendix A.

**Sarcasm classification training set, SarcTrain (10,000, all human).** The dataset is balanced across four language × label buckets, with 2,500 instances per bucket. Three buckets are downsampled; the Chinese sarcastic bucket starts from 2,396 records representing 2,394 unique texts and adds 104 oversampled records. The resulting set is organized into five folds.

**MGTD training set, AuthTrain (19,995).** The human side is sampled directly from SarcTrain with language balance. On the AI side, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (Ollama tag `deepseek-r1:8b`) is instructed to produce a topically related output from each human seed: 9,996 outputs are retained for 9,999 human instances (99.97% pairing coverage), while three generation failures remain only on the human side. Four templates (Chinese/English × sarcastic/normal) follow the balanced seed labels. Authorship and requested-style labels are constructed as separate, approximately balanced factors; no independence is assumed for realized textual features or perceived sarcasm. Generation parameters, full templates, and post-processing steps are given in Appendix A.

**Generation training set, GenTrain (2,428; used in §6).** From 5,257 eligible positive-sarcasm records in iSarcasm and ToSarcasm, seed-42 sampling selects 2,103 records (1,214 English; 889 Chinese). Chinese oversampling adds 325 records, yielding 1,214 per language. By prompt type, the set contains 374 rewriting, 839 response, and 1,215 comment instances and serves as the instruction-tuning data for SFT.

**Diagnostic test set, DualTest (4,664).** This is the core data contribution of the paper: each instance bears both `is_sarcastic` and `is_human` labels. The human side comprises the official iSarcasm test split (1,400 English instances) and the ToSarcasm test set (972 Chinese instances). Generation and quality control retain deepseek-r1:8b counterparts for 2,292 of the 2,372 human texts (96.6% coverage). Human texts retain their annotations; for AI texts, `is_sarcastic` records the prompted condition rather than an independently annotated judgment of perceived sarcasm, and `is_human` is false by construction. After combining languages, the four quadrants contain 823 human × sarcastic, 1,549 human × non-sarcastic, 779 AI × sarcastic, and 1,513 AI × non-sarcastic instances (see Appendix A for the language × source × sarcasm distribution).

## 4 Dual-Head Detection Model <!-- budget: ~0.9 pages -->

### 4.1 Tasks and Architecture

Given a Chinese or English input text $x$, the model produces two separate, non-mutually-exclusive binary predictions: whether $x$ is labeled sarcastic and whether its authorship source is labeled human or AI. Any combination of the two labels is valid. The tasks share a single DeBERTa-v3-large encoder (He et al., 2023; this encoder was pretrained on English corpora, and §5.1 directly compares it with multilingual-pretrained mBERT / XLM-R on the same bilingual test set). Encoder outputs are aggregated by an intermediate module and then passed to two task-specific classification heads; Figure 1 shows the RCNN configuration.

![Figure 1](figures/fig_architecture.png)

**Figure 1: RCNN dual-head configuration: a shared DeBERTa-v3-large encoder, an RCNN intermediate module (dashed), and two task-specific heads. The comparisons in §5 remove this component in the plain configuration or replace it with task-branch interaction projection, while retaining the same encoder backbone and task definitions (Appendix B).**

The RCNN configuration uses BiLSTM + CNN as its intermediate module. A single-layer bidirectional LSTM with hidden size 256 per direction produces a 512-dimensional output at each token position. We concatenate it with the 1,024-dimensional encoder output, then apply three groups of one-dimensional convolutions with kernel widths 3/4/5 and 128 channels each. Global max pooling produces a 384-dimensional vector $z$; after dropout, $z$ is passed to the two heads. This module adapts the mBERT-RCNN intermediate architecture of Yang and Ikeda (2024) to a DeBERTa-based dual-head setting. The plain variant sends `[CLS]` directly to both heads. The task-branch interaction projection variant forms two learned 256-dimensional projections, $h_s$ and $h_a$, and updates them as $h_s'=h_s+P_{a\rightarrow s}(h_a)$ and $h_a'=h_a+P_{s\rightarrow a}(h_s)$. Each branch contains one vector, so no query–key interaction remains; $P$ is the effective learned value/output projection. The configurations retain the same encoder backbone and task definitions, but their intermediate representations, head input dimensions, and parameter counts differ; remaining dimensions and dropout settings are in Appendix B.

### 4.2 Training Protocol

Training uses a mixed loader that alternates batches by task: each batch contains samples from only one task, and MGTD and sarcasm batches alternate in a 2:1 ratio, matching the data-size ratio between AuthTrain and SarcTrain. Let the shared parameters be $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ (the encoder and intermediate module), let $z(x)$ be their aggregated representation for input $x$, and let $W_s$ and $W_a$ denote the two classification heads. At step $t$, the loss is computed only for the head corresponding to the task of the current batch $B_t$:

$$
\mathcal{L}(B_t)=
\begin{cases}
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}_{w}\bigl(\mathrm{softmax}(W_s\,z(x)),\,y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{SarcTrain},\\[8pt]
\dfrac{1}{|B_t|}\displaystyle\sum_{(x,y)\in B_t}\mathrm{CE}\bigl(\mathrm{softmax}(W_a\,z(x)),\,y\bigr), & B_t\subset \text{AuthTrain},
\end{cases}
$$

where $\mathrm{CE}_w$ is class-weighted cross-entropy that compensates for the scarcity of sarcastic positives (weights are provided in Appendix B). The gradient flow follows directly from the equation: on sarcasm batches, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_a = 0$ (symmetrically, $\partial\mathcal{L}/\partial W_s = 0$ on MGTD batches), whereas $\theta_{\mathrm{sh}}$ receives gradients from both types of batches. The shared encoder and intermediate module are shaped jointly by both tasks, while each classification head is updated only by data from its own task.

Optimization uses separate learning rates for parameter groups: the pretrained encoder is updated at a much lower rate than the intermediate module and classification heads to preserve pretrained representations. Model selection accounts for both tasks. The early-stopping signal at epoch $e$ is the equally weighted sum of each task's validation loss normalized by its first-epoch value:

$$
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{stop}}^{(e)}=\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(1)}}+\tfrac{1}{2}\,\frac{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}}{\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(1)}},
$$

where $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$ and $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$ are the epoch-$e$ validation losses for the sarcasm and MGTD tasks on their respective validation sets. Normalization prevents early stopping from being dominated by the larger MGTD task.

Training and evaluation follow a “five independent runs with a fixed external evaluation set” protocol. SarcTrain is pre-split into five folds: run $k$ trains the sarcasm task on four folds, with the held-out fold as the sarcasm validation set providing $\mathcal{L}_{s}^{(e)}$; AuthTrain uses a fixed 90% training / 10% validation split shared by all runs, its validation portion providing $\mathcal{L}_{a}^{(e)}$, so between-run variation is concentrated in changes to the sarcasm training data. Each run is trained from a fresh initialization of the same pretrained base; all architectures and ablations share the fold assignments and seed scheme, and cross-model comparisons are paired by split. The five models are then evaluated on both tasks on the external test set DualTest, which is involved in no split. The main tables report the mean ± standard deviation across the five runs (hereafter, five-run mean ± standard deviation); this standard deviation reflects between-run variation from training splits and initialization, while test-set sampling uncertainty is quantified separately through confidence intervals for the final model (Table 3 and Appendix C.7).

A separate final model, used only for subsequent inference and rescoring (§5.3 quadrant diagnosis and §6 scoring), is trained on all of SarcTrain and all of AuthTrain with the same hyperparameters and a fixed number of epochs (the mode of the five runs' best early-stopping epochs + 1). Its weights and training duration are not selected using DualTest, and its results do not enter the main tables. Class weights, sampling ratio, grouped learning rates, and full hyperparameters are provided in Appendix B.

## 5 Experiments and Diagnosis <!-- budget: ~2.9 pages -->

We first descriptively compare encoder backbones and intermediate configurations on DualTest. We then compare matched dual- and single-head models and partition the same evaluation into four “source × sarcasm” quadrants. DualTest is used for both the descriptive architecture comparison and subgroup diagnosis, but not for training, validation, threshold selection, or epoch selection. Overall metrics below are five-run means ± standard deviations; quadrant metrics are fixed-final-model point estimates, with paired comparisons made between models on the same subgroup texts.

### 5.1 Backbone Comparison

We compare dual-head models across four encoder backbones (mBERT, XLM-RoBERTa-base, and DeBERTa-v3 base and large) and, on the large encoder, the two intermediate-module variants of §4.1. The encoders span multilingual and English-centric pretraining: mBERT is BERT-base pretrained on multilingual Wikipedia (Devlin et al., 2019), XLM-RoBERTa-base is pretrained on CommonCrawl text covering about one hundred languages (Conneau et al., 2020), and DeBERTa-v3 combines disentangled attention with ELECTRA-style pretraining on primarily English corpora. “Sarcasm F1” is the F1 of the sarcastic class (positive class), following the official iSarcasmEval convention, and “MGTD F1” is the F1 of the human class (positive class); both are computed over the full DualTest set, aggregating Chinese and English and both source sides, with AI-side sarcasm labels constructed from the generation instructions (§3).

### 5.3 Four-Quadrant Diagnosis: Class-Conditional Negative Transfer across Intermediate Architectures

Aggregate metrics obscure the structure of errors. The overall sarcasm F1 of the plain dual-head model alone would merely suggest mediocre performance, although its errors are concentrated on AI text. We partition DualTest by source × sarcasm labels and examine the predictions separately. Human labels are dataset annotations, whereas AI labels record prompted conditions. Because each quadrant contains only one reference class, we report recall for sarcastic subsets and the false-positive rate for non-sarcastic subsets.

The single-task control and cross-generator replication establish the behavioral conclusion: class-conditional negative transfer exists and is induced by the complete joint-training procedure. Representation-level tests support the explanatory hypothesis that the sarcasm head exploits a source-correlated shortcut while showing that the prediction shift is sensitive to high-variance directions whose semantic content remains unresolved.

#### 5.3.1 Three Architectures and Single-Head Controls

We compare five detectors on the same DualTest set. The three dual-head variants characterize how the observed failure changes with intermediate structure. The two single-head models provide matched controls for estimating the effect of adding MGTD supervision. If training the sarcasm task only on human text were sufficient to produce the failure, a matched single-head model should show a comparable pattern; if the large increase appears only after MGTD training is added, it is induced by the joint-training procedure. Table 3 reports sarcasm predictions; corresponding MGTD source-class metrics are in Appendix C.

**Table 3: Comparison of five sarcasm classifiers on four source × sarcasm quadrants (recall for sarcastic subsets; false-positive rate for non-sarcastic subsets; human labels are annotations and AI labels are prompted conditions)**

| Model (sarcasm prediction) | Human × sarcastic recall | Human × non-sarcastic false positive | AI × sarcastic recall | **AI × non-sarcastic false positive** |
|------------|----------------|------------------|--------------|--------------------|
| Plain dual-head | 67.1% | 32.9% | 98.2% | **86.3%** [84.5, 88.0] |
| Interaction-projection dual-head | 71.9% | 37.6% | 94.4% | **40.9%** [38.5, 43.4] |
| RCNN dual-head | 80.8% | 40.5% | 89.3% | **34.1%** [31.8, 36.5] |
| Plain single-head (sarcasm only) | 67.8% | 28.7% | 76.6% | **19.3%** [17.4, 21.4] |
| RCNN single-head (sarcasm only) | 66.2% | 28.5% | 79.1% | **19.3%** [17.4, 21.4] |

Brackets in the final column give Wilson 95% confidence intervals over test instances. Intervals for all quadrants, paired tests on the same texts, and score distributions for each quadrant (showing that the failure is an overall rightward shift of the score distribution rather than a threshold artifact) are provided in Appendix C.7.

The plain dual-head model predicts sarcasm for 86.3% of AI × non-sarcastic instances. Both intermediate dual-head variants are more than 45 pp lower, and paired tests compare their predictions on the same texts (Appendix C.7). RCNN is about 7 pp lower than interaction projection; because interaction projection has no matched single-head counterpart, its result is an across-configuration observation rather than a matched estimate of buffering. The matched single-head models have a false-positive rate of 19.3%. The plain dual–single difference on the same texts is significant by McNemar test (discordant pairs 1,021 / 7, $p < 0.001$). The absence of AI texts from SarcTrain alone does not reproduce the large increase; it appears when MGTD training is added. In this subgroup and for the evaluated generators, RCNN does not reduce the single-task rate but is associated with a smaller rate under joint training.

On the same AI × non-sarcastic subset, the MGTD head's false-positive rate is 6.1% (plain) / 6.6% (RCNN), and no analogous extreme subgroup error appears under its source-class metrics. Within AI texts, AUC remains 0.82–0.88 across models while joint training raises the sarcasm-score level and largely preserves internal ordering. The harm is therefore a source-conditioned upward score shift concentrated on the non-sarcastic prompted subset, which we term class-conditional negative transfer.

#### 5.3.2 Mechanism Hypothesis and Representation-Level Diagnostics

Based on the behavioral evidence in §5.3.1, we propose a mechanism hypothesis. Under the gradient flow of §4.2, the shared encoder and intermediate module receive MGTD gradients, while the sarcasm head is updated only on the all-human SarcTrain and never receives sarcasm supervision on AI text. Reading a representation shaped by MGTD, it may misuse source-collinear cues that are not intrinsic to sarcasm as sarcasm signals. Under this interpretation, an intermediate module may partially buffer changes in the shared representation; this remains a hypothesis rather than an established causal mechanism.

We test the hypothesis on the frozen plain dual-head model; Appendix E records the full protocol and self-checks. A linear probe decodes source at AUC 0.986, but the matched single-head representation also reaches 0.974, so decodability alone cannot explain the joint-training-specific failure. The MGTD-head AI logit correlates with the sarcasm-head logit on AI × non-sarcastic text (Spearman 0.52), while the MGTD-head and probe directions align with the sarcasm direction at $|\cos| \approx$ 0.41–0.49 in whitened geometry. LEACE reduces measured linear source decodability from AUC 0.982 to 0.589 and the false-positive rate from 84.8% to 68.7%. However, removing a high-variance direction orthogonal to the estimated linear source direction leaves measured linear decodability unchanged (0.982) while similarly reducing the rate to 66.7%; ten sampled random-direction controls show no comparable effect. The interventions therefore indicate sensitivity to high-variance directions without isolating source identity as causal.

The representation-level tests therefore support and constrain the source-shortcut hypothesis: the failure is sensitive to a dominant-variance subspace and collinear with source, but the semantic content of the effective directions and the source concept's specific causal role remain unresolved. The orthogonal control demonstrates that “decodable, correlated, and responsive to intervention” does not by itself establish a mechanism. Irrespective of the preferred interpretation, the false-positive-rate differences among the three configurations remain observational facts.

#### 5.3.3 Cross-Generator Replication

To test whether the failure depends on a particular generator, we regenerate the AI side of DualTest with Qwen3.5-9B (Ollama tag `qwen3.5:9b`) while keeping the human texts unchanged. The replication contains 1,550 outputs under the non-sarcastic instruction. The plain dual-head false-positive rate rises from 86.3% to 91.3%, while the matched single-head rate falls from 19.3% to 7.9%, increasing the gap from 67.0 to 83.4 pp. RCNN and interaction projection remain below the plain dual-head rate (22.1% and 29.5%). The second generator therefore reproduces the direction of the instruction-conditioned dual–single subgroup gap, although its magnitude differs.

## 6 Exploratory Comparison of Detector Scores and LLM-Judge Ratings <!-- budget: ~0.4 pages -->

This section asks a related but separate question: whether a source-classification probability can serve as a proxy for perceived human-likeness, and whether the detector's sarcasm score tracks external LLM ratings. These are comparisons between different constructs; a classification boundary need not measure style realization or generation quality. The failure in §5 may contribute to disagreement but is not treated as its sole cause.

We use Qwen3-8B (Qwen Team, 2025) as the base model and fine-tune it on GenTrain using QLoRA (Dettmers et al., 2023). The 300 prompts comprise 180 Chinese topic-comment tasks and 120 English rewriting tasks, so language is confounded with generation task. Base and SFT outputs are scored by the RCNN detector and by three LLM judges (DeepSeek-V3, Gemini-2.5-Flash-Lite, and GPT-5-nano) that are not shown the ground-truth source.

Agreement is dimension- and language-dependent. In Chinese, SFT raises the detector's sarcasm score from 0.780 to 0.895 but lowers the mean judge rating from 4.01 to 3.71; Chinese sarcasm correlation is $-0.020$, whereas English correlation is $+0.338$. The MGTD head assigns higher human-authorship probabilities to SFT outputs in both languages while the judges assign lower perceived human-likeness ratings. Their pooled correlation is $-0.212$, driven mainly by opposing condition-level changes; within-condition correlations are near zero or weakly negative. Instance-level agreement among the judges is low to moderate, so the evidence supports condition-level directional disagreement rather than a reliable per-text reference ranking. The plain-detector control changes correlations by at most 0.02 (Appendix C).

## 7 Conclusion <!-- budget: ~0.45 pages -->

This paper identifies class-conditional negative transfer after MGTD training is added. Relative to a same-architecture single-head control using the same sarcasm data, the plain dual-head model has a sharply higher false-positive rate on AI text generated under non-sarcastic instructions. A second generator reproduces the gap's direction but not its magnitude, and both intermediate configurations have lower rates than plain dual-head. Representation diagnostics link the shift to source-correlated, high-variance directions while revealing error redistribution rather than overall improvement. Detector probabilities and LLM-judge ratings also differ by dimension and task–language setting, motivating external validation before the evaluated scores are used as generation-quality signals.

## Limitations <!-- mandatory; excluded from page limit; must not contain new results -->

The first set of limitations concerns data and experimental settings. The AI-side labels in DualTest are instruction-conditioned rather than independently annotated, so an apparent false positive can reflect detector error or realized sarcasm despite a non-sarcastic instruction. Generation failures leave 80 human texts without retained main-generator counterparts, and seven exact train–test matches remain on the AI side. MGTD training uses one generator, while the second generator replaces only DualTest's AI side. The result is a directional replication across two retained output sets rather than broad generator-family coverage. The five source corpora also operationalize sarcasm differently, and the detector does not use conversational context.

The second set concerns comparison design and mechanism depth. DualTest supports both descriptive architecture comparison and subgroup diagnosis. The positive-class weight can affect absolute false-positive rates, although each matched dual–single comparison uses the same weight. Dual and single procedures differ in task schedule, total shared-encoder updates, and early stopping, so they estimate the complete joint-training procedure rather than a compute-matched auxiliary-gradient effect. Cross-architecture comparisons are not capacity- or optimization-matched, their MGTD-head learning rates differ, and interaction projection lacks a matched single-head control. Representation diagnostics use one final plain dual-head model; source is also decodable from its single-head counterpart, and the interventions mainly redistribute non-sarcastic errors across sources rather than lowering their average.

The third set concerns the scale and proxy nature of generation evaluation. Human-authorship probability and perceived human-likeness are related but non-equivalent constructs. Section 6 uses three LLM judges rather than human evaluators, and their instance-level agreement is low to moderate. The Chinese and English subsets differ in sample size, generation task, prompt structure, and source data. The analysis therefore shows condition-level directional differences between the evaluated detector scores and selected LLM-judge ratings, not human-perceived quality or a pure language effect. Priority extensions are independent annotation of AI-side style realization, capacity- and budget-matched controls, and multi-generator, multilingual, and calibration analyses.

Generation instructions use four templates defined by language × style, with style determined by the seed instance's sarcasm label. The templates constrain output style and length (20–80 Chinese characters; 10–50 English words). The complete Chinese sarcastic template used in the experiment is reproduced verbatim below:

```
【任务】生成一条讽刺风格的评论
【主题】{seed}
【要求】
- 直接给出简短的评论，不要解释思路或分析
- 风格要讽刺、幽默、夸张
- 表达与主题相关的观点即可，不必追求严谨
- 20-80字为佳
【评论】
```

Its English translation is:

```
[Task] Generate a sarcastic-style comment
[Topic] {seed}
[Requirements]
- Directly provide a short comment; do not explain your reasoning or analysis
- The style should be sarcastic, humorous, and exaggerated
- It is sufficient to express a viewpoint related to the topic; strict rigor is not required
- Preferably 20–80 Chinese characters
[Comment]
```

The Chinese non-sarcastic version changes only the style line to “The style should be normal and natural.” The two English templates parallel the Chinese templates (the style lines are “sarcastic, humorous, exaggerated” and “normal, natural,” respectively; length constraint: 10–50 words). Post-processing comprises three steps: removing `<think>…</think>` chain-of-thought segments; removing reasoning-preface terms such as “好的 / 嗯 / Okay / Let me / First”; and removing Markdown markup. Failed generations are discarded. AuthTrain contains three generation failures (9,996 complete pairs; the corresponding human instances are retained). The AI side of the main DualTest set likewise excludes a small number of failures; cross-generator replication has zero failures (hence 1,550 AI × non-sarcastic instances for the replication set versus 1,513 for the main set). For cross-generator replication, thinking mode is explicitly disabled for the reasoning model (Ollama `think = false`) to prevent the reasoning process from exhausting the output budget. Inline chain-of-thought from deepseek-r1:8b is removed through the post-processing described above.

**A.5 SFT training and generation-evaluation set.** Eligibility filtering retains 5,257 positive-sarcasm records suitable for generation tasks (2,972 English; 2,285 Chinese). By prompt type, it comprises 374 rewriting, 839 response, and 1,215 comment instances. Generation evaluation uses an independent set of 300 prompts (180 Chinese and 120 English): the Chinese task is to generate a sarcastic comment on a given topic, and the English task is to rewrite a normal sentence as a sarcastic one. The detector scoring and three blinded LLM evaluations in §6 are both conducted on this set.

## Appendix E: Preliminary Representation-Level Analysis of the Failure Mechanism <!-- excluded from page limit; two columns -->

This appendix records the complete protocol, data, and self-checks for the representation-level tests in §5.3.2. The interpretation of conclusions follows §5.3.2.

**E.1 Setup.** All analyses use the frozen model that produces the 86.3% result for the plain dual-head model in the main text, without updating its parameters. We use split-sample evaluation: the direction is estimated on the fitting half and metrics are calculated on the evaluation half, so the appendix baseline (84.8%) differs slightly from the full-set value (86.3%). Code self-checks: manually computed logits match model outputs elementwise (maximum error $1.4 \times 10^{-6}$); the erasure-projection residual is approximately $10^{-6}$; after LEACE the fitting-half class-mean difference has relative residual $7 \times 10^{-6}$ and an in-sample linear probe is at chance (AUC 0.514); probe selectivity is 0.986 with true labels and 0.512 with permuted labels.

**E.2 Protocol.** Section 5.3.2 gives the principles and citations for each test; implementation details are recorded here. The linear probe is logistic regression with five-fold cross-validation. The selectivity control retrains the same probe with permuted source labels. Correlation analyses are rechecked after stratification by text-length tertile. For alignment, $\Sigma$ is the Ledoit–Wolf covariance fitted to all pooled DualTest representations, with eigenvalues clipped below $10^{-8}$; $w$ denotes a task-head weight difference or probe direction. Whitened alignment is $\cos(\Sigma^{1/2}w_1,\Sigma^{1/2}w_2)$. Erasure interventions include a single direction, the INLP subspace ($k = 1$–$16$), and LEACE. Controls comprise ten sampled random whitened directions and erasure after re-estimating directions from permuted labels. srcORTH-PC1 is orthogonalized against the estimated linear source direction; unchanged linear source decodability does not imply independence from all multidimensional or nonlinear source information.

**E.3 Diagnostic evidence.** Source is highly linearly decodable: AUC 0.986 (sarcastic subset: 0.990 / non-sarcastic subset: 0.985; permuted-label control: 0.512; single-head model: 0.974, meaning that decodability is not unique to joint training). On AI × non-sarcastic instances, the MGTD-head AI logit and sarcasm-head logit correlate at $\rho = 0.519$ (0.44/0.56/0.63 by text-length tertile). The sarcasm decision direction and source direction are nearly orthogonal in the original geometry ($\cos \approx -0.03$) but moderately aligned in whitened geometry ($|\cos| = 0.41$ when measured using the probe direction / 0.49 using the MGTD-head direction). All are compatible with the shortcut hypothesis, but all constitute correlational evidence.

**E.4 Intervention evidence.** $\text{gap} =$ AI × non-sarcastic false-positive rate − human × non-sarcastic false-positive rate; source AUC is the decoding ability of a probe retrained after erasure.

| Erasure scheme | AI × non-sarcastic false positive | Human × non-sarcastic false positive | Gap | Source AUC (after) |
|----------|----------------|------------------|------|----------------|
| None (baseline) | 84.8 | 32.8 | 52.0 | 0.982 |
| Single direction (MGTD head / independent probe / random) | 84.8–86.2 | — | — | ≈ 0.98 |
| INLP subspace ($k = 16$) | 89.8 | — | — | 0.974 |
| **LEACE (linear erasure)** | 68.7 | 49.5 | 19.1 | **0.589** |
| Sampled random whitened ×10 (control) | 84.9 | 32.7 | — | 0.982 |
| **srcORTH-PC1 (high variance, orthogonal to the estimated linear source direction)** | **66.7** | 54.1 | 12.6 | **0.982** |

Interpretation: single-direction and low-dimensional INLP erasure produce little change. LEACE reduces measured linear source decodability and the source-specific gap from 52 to 19, while the ten sampled random-direction controls show no comparable gap change. Erasing a high-variance direction orthogonal to the estimated linear source direction changes the AI-side rate to 66.7 and the gap to 12.6 while leaving measured linear source decodability unchanged. This shows sensitivity to at least one high-variance direction, not an independent or unique causal subspace. Under LEACE, the unweighted mean false-positive rate across the two non-sarcastic source groups is almost unchanged (58.8 → 59.1), indicating error redistribution rather than overall improvement.
